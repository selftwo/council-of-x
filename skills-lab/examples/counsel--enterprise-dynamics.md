# Counsel memo: shipping alone is not enough in an enterprise

## The challenge

You are a strong solo AI builder. You ship fast, you care about design taste, and you get leverage from tools rather than headcount. The gap is everything around the shipping: managing people, reading social dynamics, and operating inside enterprise environments where politics, stakeholders, and visibility decide what happens to your work. The risk is not that you build the wrong thing. The risk is that you build the right thing and it dies quietly because nobody with power understood it, sponsored it, or heard about it. The counsel below is pulled from Lenny's Podcast guests who spoke directly to this situation: senior individual contributors and builders who had to learn organizational skill as a discipline, not as a personality trait.

## 1. Treat managing up as a senior skill, not a junior chore

Wes Kao's core argument is that managing up is not something you graduate out of. It is the skill that gets people promoted, and the people above you are above you partly because they do it well. In practice it means: find out what worries your boss, take those things off their plate, and communicate proactively so they never have to chase you for status. For a solo builder this is cheap leverage. You already produce artifacts constantly; point some of that output upward, on a regular cadence, framed around your boss's concerns rather than your build log.

Who said it: Wes Kao, "Become a better communicator: Specific frameworks to improve your clarity, influence, and impact" (April 2025).

Quote: "the most senior people are best at managing up," Wes Kao. Timestamp: 31:58.

## 2. Run the Magic Loop to convert good work into standing

Ethan Evans built his Magic Loop at Amazon for exactly the case where the environment does not hand you growth: do your job well, ask your manager how you can help, do what they say, then ask for work that helps them and also moves you toward your goal. It works because it aligns your extra effort with your manager's interests instead of hoping merit gets noticed. Evans is explicit that this is designed for imperfect situations, including a mediocre or overloaded manager, which is common in large enterprises. The point for you: stop waiting for the organization to route opportunity to the best builder. Create the exchange yourself, and keep it in your control.

Who said it: Ethan Evans, "Taking control of your career" (January 2024).

Quote: "managers help those who help them. It's just human nature," Ethan Evans. Timestamp: 00:07:14.

## 3. Accept that stakeholder management is the job, and stop running it through one-on-ones

Camille Fournier spent years running internal platform teams, the most enterprise-political job in engineering, and her blunt take is that stakeholder management is a real skill set you must build, not an annoyance to route around. Her tactical advice: do not manage many stakeholders through private one-on-ones. When one stakeholder is unhappy and five are happy, one-on-ones mean the unhappy one never hears the others, so your reassurance sounds like excuses. Put stakeholders in shared forums where they hear each other's reality directly. For a solo builder in an enterprise, this replaces charisma with structure: you do not need to win each person over privately, you need a room where the support you already have becomes visible.

Who said it: Camille Fournier, "The things engineers are desperate for PMs to understand" (September 2024).

Quote: "your unhappy stakeholders aren't hearing that," Camille Fournier. Timestamp: 00:43:07.

## 4. Build influence in public, through writing

Brandon Chu became a VP at Shopify partly through his public writing about product management, and he found something counterintuitive: publishing externally moved things internally. His posts built trust with the CEO and drew talent to the company, which gave him internal standing no meeting could. He also argues you owe your peers and stakeholders written clarity, because writing forces you to understand your own position before you argue it. This is the visibility path built for you. You are a maker, not a networker, so make artifacts that argue for your work: internal memos, demos with written narratives, or public posts. Let the writing circulate in rooms you are not in.

Who said it: Brandon Chu, "Building product at Shopify, how writing changed the trajectory of his career" (June 2022).

Quote: "writing externally and getting momentum externally was a better way to influence internally," Brandon Chu. Timestamp: 33:20.

## 5. With difficult colleagues, get curious about what they want

Anneka Gupta's method for difficult colleagues has two parts. First, find out what the person actually wants, and if you cannot read them directly, ask people around them about their motivations, then connect what they want to what you are trying to achieve. Second, replace frustration with curiosity: study what the difficult person is good at, because the emotional shift from resentment to learning keeps you effective in the interaction instead of defensive. For someone who finds social dynamics opaque, this is useful because it turns politics into a research problem, and research is something you are already good at. Motivations are discoverable facts, not vibes.

Who said it: Anneka Gupta, "Becoming more strategic, navigating difficult colleagues, harnessing founder mode, and more" (October 2024).

Quote: "everyone has something that they can teach you," Anneka Gupta. Timestamp: 00:39:08.

## 6. Deepen work relationships with the 15% rule

Carole Robin, who taught interpersonal dynamics at Stanford GSB for years, teaches that relationships deepen through reciprocal disclosure: if you hold your cards close, the other person holds theirs closer. Her practical tool is the 15% rule. You have a comfort zone and a danger zone, and learning happens just outside the comfort zone, so disclose about 15% more than feels comfortable, see how the other person responds, and let a new, larger comfort zone form. She also stresses appropriate disclosure: showing uncertainty and asking for help builds credibility, while dumping unfiltered doubt destroys it. For a builder who defaults to transactional interactions, this gives a measured, low-risk protocol for building the trust that enterprise influence runs on.

Who said it: Carole Robin, "How to build deeper, more robust relationships" (April 2024).

Quote: "step a little bit outside your comfort zone," Carole Robin. Timestamp: 00:22:57.
