# Engagement

Engagement is the center-piece of growth. All the efforts lead
to engagement, from acquisition → setup → core action →
habit building(engagement). Even resurrecting users who have
moved out of the product leads back to engagement.

Engagement has a positive impact on all important metrics –
acquisition, retention, monetisation. 

```
Higher engagement leads to higher acquisition, either
through organic or referrals. A higher engagement on the
platform can increase organic presence by helping it rank
higher in search results. It could also lead to people who
are highly engaged recommending the product to others.  
Higher engagement would lead to stronger habits, and that
just means users automatically coming back to the product
without thinking much.
Higher engagement leads to higher monetisation. In the
ad-based products like Instagram, more engagement
means more opportunities to show ads and make money. In
the transaction or productivity platforms, higher
engagement represents higher value that the user gets. As
a result of higher value, the user would retain as a loyal
subscriber to the platform, thereby increasing LTV. In many
cases, the user would also upgrade themselves to a higher
tier of subscription, again impacting monetisation
positively.
```

# Analyzing engagement

To analyze and understand engagement, we need to segment
users based on their overall engagement. This segmentation
helps us see the behavioral patterns.

A simple segmentation can be in terms of casual, core and
power users.

## Casual, core and power buckets

If we look at the engagement of a particular product in terms of
time spent, we may get a normal distribution curve where there
are fewer users at both ends of the curve and more number of
users in between.

# Improving Habit Strength

After we have defined the habit metric, we need to look at how
we can improve the habit. Here are key ways to improve the
habit metric:

1. Segment to find the right target market
2. Solve adjacent, frequent problems
3. Identify and build external triggers
4. Solve a problem in a memorable way
5. Use continuous rewards

# Building Cues

Triggers can be internal or external. Let’s take a few examples. 

For example, when someone is bored (emotional state), they
take an action to open Instagram and entertain themselves.
After doing this, they’re rewarded by finding more content that
interests them. This is an example of an internal trigger. 

What about someone sending us the invite for a Zoom
meeting? That is a pretty strong external trigger. Even if we
aren’t using Zoom, we will be compelled to give it a try. 

The goal of habit formation is to create internal triggers
through repetition. For example, as our habit of using Zoom as
a meeting tool becomes automatic, we don’t need an external
trigger. We would be using the tool automatically whenever we
think of doing a remote meeting. 

All internal, habitual triggers fit into one of five categories:
Location
Time
Emotional State (facing a problem, having a question, or in
a situation)
Other People

# Continuous Rewards

In the hooked model, Nir Eyal proposes variable rewards to
build habits. The Hook Model is a four-step process that
businesses can use to create habit-building products.

The Hook Model consists of the following 4 steps. The first 3 are
a variation of cue-action-reward:

1. Trigger
2. Action
3. Variable Reward: The rewards here are variable rather than
    predictable. Think of opening the Facebook app to check
    how many likes your photo got. The number of likes is
    variable, thereby reinforcing the behavior more strongly
    than predictable rewards. 
4. Investment: An action from the user that creates switching
    cost for the end user, like creating a profile and adding
    photos. 

While the hooked model is useful once the habit is formed,
research shows that in the early stages of building a habit,
continuous rewards are more effective in building a behavior. 

```
Courses
In a continuous reward schedule, a reward is provided every
single time a desired action is done by the user, resulting in a
strong association between the behavior and its consequences
in the user’s mind.
Beyond the continuous schedule, we also need to figure out
what category of reward makes more sense for our product. 
```

# The Science of Habit Building

The science of building habits is pretty fascinating. Such
complex behaviors of humans come down to three simple steps:

1. Cue - trigger to perform an action
2. Routine - performing the action
3. Reward - reward when the action is performed

it hunger? Boredom? Low blood sugar? Or is it that you need a
break before plunging into another task? 

```

Other People

# Defining the Habit Metric

The habit metric is difficult to define because of two reasons,
The time taken to build habits varies depending on the
habit. Some take months to form whereas others get
formed in weeks.  
The time taken is also person-dependent. People take
different time and effort to form the same habit. 

So if you are looking at your product and asking how to define
when the habit has been formed for the product, it is trickier to
answer because of the two reasons listed above.

```
Courses
So how do we define it? Let’s first look at how scientists define
it while studying habits.
To gauge how much time it takes to build a habit, scientists
look at the automaticity of the new behavior, i.e. how much
time does it take for people to do things automatically without
an explicit reminder. 
In 2009, Lally and colleagues from University College London
recruited 96 people who were interested in forming a new habit
such as eating a piece of fruit with lunch or doing a 15-minute
run each day. 
Participants were then asked daily how automatic their chosen
habits felt. These questions checked whether the potential
habit was ‘hard not to do’ and could be done ‘without thinking’.
When the researchers examined the different habits, many of
the participants showed a curved relationship between practice
and automaticity depicted below (solid line). A plateau on the
curve means more repetition from the point forward and
doesn't strengthen the habit anymore.
On an average, a plateau in automaticity was reached after 66
days. But it takes much less time to take a glass of water
everyday (20 days), than doing 50 sit-ups (>100 days).
```
```
So the more difficult the task is, the more repetitions we need
to form automaticity.

# Working Backwards from

# Long-term Retention

The goal of habit formation is long-term retention (LTR). So we
can work backwards from long-term retention while defining
the habit metric. How do we do that?

We need to look at the correlation between N repeats of the
core action, and LTR. If the correlation is strong, we can push
users to perform N repeats. Further, we need to time bound the
repeats to create a good metric.

The habit metric can be defined as NaT where N is the number
of core actions (a) done within the time T. 

Let’s take a look at what this metric could look like for different
types of products.

```
Instagram is an attention app. The need for entertainment
and connecting with people is a daily need. The habit
metric would be something like spending >10 mins on the
app for 5 out of 7 days (5a7D)
```
```
Courses
Compare this to a workplace product like Slack, where the
need is checking and sending messages 5 to 6 days a week.
A habit metric might be something similar to 5a7D in days,
where a is sending > 1 message per day. 
For a transaction product like Amazon, a habit metric
could be 3a3M where 3M represents 3 months. So a user
doing at least one transaction every month for 3 months
has built a habit.
```

There are two important things to remember while calculating
the habit metric.

Correlation coefficient can lie anywhere between -1 to +1, but
we should ignore weak correlation. A good threshold for
correlation is 0.4

The core actions with correlation higher than 0.4 should be
shortlisted for NaT analysis. Conversely, anything below -0.4
means that those actions are turning people off, and you should
look at that action as a reason people are churning out.

# Correlation and Causation

```
For Natura, we can see that 3a3M is a good habit metric. But
correlation doesn’t imply causation. Once we have established
correlation, the next step is to try to move users towards the
metric and see if it improves long-term retention to establish
causation. 
Causation can be done through experimentation where we
actually get a fraction of people to this habit moment metric
via features or offers. We can then look at their long term
retention and figure out whether getting more people to habit
metric leads to long-term retention.

# Summary

```
Habit formation is essential for long-term retention of a
frequent product. To understand habit formation, we
went into the science of habit building and realized that
we need to focus on cue-action-reward to actually make
or break a habit.
When it comes to defining the habit metric of the
product, we need to look at what actions affect long-
term retention (LTR). Further, we can look at the # of
repeats of the actions that have high correlation with
LTR.
As a rule of thumb, we are looking for actions with r > 0.4
or r < -0.4
