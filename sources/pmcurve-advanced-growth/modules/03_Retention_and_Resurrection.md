the highest volume of users. So teams can have a tendency to
target these because a small percentage of a large number
converted can still have a decent impact.

The hidden cost of resurrection campaigns however lies in
channel effectiveness and brand reputation. 

```
A viable method to contact non-activated user base is via
emails. Email channel effectiveness can be reduced when
we contact high volume of users via emails and most of
them don’t respond, mark as spam, or unsubscribe. This
can lead to an increase in spam score of the domain from
which we are sending the email. Because of emails from
our domain marked as spam, even our genuine users can
stop receiving emails in their Inbox as it may start going
into promotions or spam.
 When a product spams a high number of people, that can
seem pushy and lead to negative word of mouth. This, in
turn, hurts future prospects for the product.

# Identifying and Managing At

# Risk Users

At risk users for your product are the ones who are prone to
disengage with the product. We need to be proactive in defining
and managing at-risk users. 

We can define at risk users by creating prediction models.
Creating a prediction model usually has multiple steps. To
simply understand prediction models, what we are trying to do
is looking at user behavior data and trying to find patterns
among the users who churn. For example, users submitting
poor satisfaction scores could be a signal of churn. 

Creating a predictive model is a multi-stage process. As always,
before going into quantitative analysis, we should try to
understand the user's mindset when it comes to churn.

So how do we evaluate which new use-cases or features to go
after? To do so, we need to understand and apply a pretty useful
mental model – jobs-to-be-done, abbreviated as JTBD. 

JTBD states that people don't buy products; they “hire” them to
do jobs, such as solving a problem or fulfilling a desire – in a
particular context.

Probably one of the most famous quotes in marketing comes
from HBS Professor Theodore Levitt: “People don’t want to buy
a quarter-inch drill. They want a quarter-inch hole!” This quote
describes JTBD well. People don’t care about products or tools,
they care about the benefits it has to offer and whether it can
meet their needs or not.

A popular case study that illustrates nuances of JTBD was
shared by Clayton Christensen, a renowned professor at HBS.

**_Hiring a Milkshake_**

_Christensen shares the story of a fast-food restaurant chain that
wanted to improve its milkshake sales. The company started by
segmenting its market both by product (milkshakes) and by
demographics. Next, the marketing department conducted a survey
to figure out an ideal milkshake for their TG (thick, thin, chunky,
smooth, fruity, chocolaty, etc.). The would-be customers answered
as honestly as they could, and the company made changes to the
product. But alas, milkshake sales did not improve._

```
Courses
The company then reached out to one of Christensen's fellow
researchers, who approached the situation by trying to deduce the
"job" that customers were "hiring" a milkshake to do. First, he spent
a full day in one of the chain's restaurants, carefully documenting
who was buying milkshakes, when they bought them, and whether
they drank them on the premises. He discovered that 40 percent of
the milkshakes were purchased first thing in the morning, by
commuters who ordered them to go.
The next morning, he returned to the restaurant and interviewed
customers who left with a milkshake in hand, asking them what job
they had hired the milkshake to do.
"Most of them, it turned out, bought the milkshake to do a similar
job," he writes. "They faced a long, boring commute and needed
something to keep that extra hand busy and to make the commute
more interesting. They weren't yet hungry, but knew that they'd be
hungry by 10 a.m.; they wanted to consume something now that
would stave off hunger until noon. And they faced constraints:
They were in a hurry, they were wearing work clothes, and they had
(at most) one free hand."
The milkshake was hired as a replacement of a bagel or doughnut
because it was relatively tidy and appetite-quenching, and because
trying to suck a thick liquid through a thin straw gave customers
something to do with their boring commute. Understanding the job
to be done, the company could then respond by creating a morning
milkshake that was even thicker (to last through a long commute)
and more interesting (with chunks of fruit) than its predecessor.
```
### Same product can do different jobs

```
The above example shows how understanding the job your
customers are hiring your product to do can improve your
chances of winning the market. But that is not to say that the
product does a single job. The rest 60% of customers who
weren’t buying the milkshake in the morning hired it for a
different reason, and a different job possibly. Uncovering
different jobs-to-be-done for the product is essential to
understand what to build next and strengthen PMF.
```
### Defining JTBD

```
While defining JTBD, we need to keep few things in mind:
```
1. When defining “job”, it is important to define both the task
    (objective) and the context in which it occurs. 

```
Courses
```

2. The job requires the right level of abstraction. 
3. Job is very different from the traditional marketing concept
    of “needs” because it entails a much higher degree of
    specificity about what you’re solving for. A need like ‘I feel
    hungry’ is too high level
4. If your definition of “job” only implies one class of
    products that can solve it, then you must move up a level.
    The "job" must allow for alternative solutions. If only
    products in the same class like milkshakes can solve the
    problem, you’re not uncovering a job.

### Checklist of defining JTBD

Here is a good checklist of defining JTBD,

```
It is not a high-level need.
It does not describe a specific way to solve a problem using
one class of products.
It describes a task that a person wants to solve or progress
that they want to achieve in life.
It describes the context in which this task occurs. That
explains why they want to do X in a specific situation.
It is formulated at a level of abstraction that sets the
boundaries but also leaves room for finding a solution.

# e. Build virtuous loops

Virtuous loops are like the growth cycle we discussed earlier.
The engagement from existing users can fuel even more
engagement in virtuous loops. There are three kinds of loops
we can see commonly:

**Data loops**

Recommendation (Amazon) is an example of data loops where
data is used to create recommendations. Users interacting with
the recommendation generate more data which the
recommendation engine can learn from. The better the
recommendation platform becomes, the more the engagement. 

**Content loops**
Content loops can be user-generated (UGC) or company-
generated (CGC). 

```
Courses
An example of UGC is Youtube where the user posting the video
creates more content for other users. Of the other users who
come, some of them can create more content thereby creating
an ever growing loop. The more the UGC, the higher the
engagement per user, and vice versa.
Another example of UGC is pinterest. In the case of Pinterest,
the UGC can also lead to SEO benefits where Google ranks some
of the content created/pinned by the users.
```
```
The example of CGC is Netflix where higher subscription
revenue generated by Netflix can be used to create more
content/shows by the company. More shows will lead to higher
engagement. 
```
```
Network effect loops
Network effect loops work because users invite other users to
improve their experience. Examples include Slack where users
inviting their colleagues can increase the value Slack generates
for them. Most of the communication and social platforms grow
organically due to network effect loops. 
More people joining leads to more engagement for the existing
users. 
Network effect loops also make it harder for the users to leave
the platform.

# f. Introduce variable rewards

# to create stronger habits

```
We discuss continuous rewards in the section of habit
formation. Once the habit is formed, variable rewards make the
behavior more sticky.
```
```
Courses
```

Variable reward means introducing unpredictability on when
the reward will appear. A famous study in this regard is the
Skinner box.

In 1930, a psychologist at Harvard University called B.F. Skinner
made a box and placed a hungry rat inside it. The box had a
lever on one side. As the rat moved about it would accidentally
knock the lever and, when it did so, a food pellet would drop
into the box. After a rat had been put in the box a few times, it
learned to go straight to the lever and press it: the reward
reinforced the behavior. Skinner proposed that the same
principle applied to any “operant”, rat or man. He called his
device the “operant conditioning chamber”. It became known as
the Skinner box.

When B.F. Skinner performed further experiments with his box,
he discovered that if the rat got the same reward each time, it
pulled the lever only when it was hungry. The way to maximize
the number of times the rat pulled the lever was to vary the
rewards it received. If it didn’t know whether it was going to get
one pellet, or none, or several when it pulled the lever, then it
pulled the lever over and over again. It became psychologically
hooked. This became known as the principle of variable
rewards.

But why does the variable reward get the rat hooked?
Dopamine.

Dopamine is a chemical produced by our brains that plays a big
role in motivating behavior. It gets released when we take a bite
of delicious food, when we have sex, when we exercise, and
when we have good social interactions.

The human brain contains four pathways in which dopamine is
produced (not getting into detail). All pathways become active

```
Courses
when anticipating or experiencing rewards. Anticipation is the
key here, which often happens when you do certain activities
that result in rewards. For rats, the action was pulling the lever
that built the anticipation. Every time a response to a stimulus
results in a reward, these associations become stronger.
The variable rewards are what makes pulling a lever in casinos
so addictive. These rewards in the internet world could be a text
message from a friend, a “like” on Instagram, or a Facebook
notification about a friend request. That’s what makes social
media apps so addictive.
