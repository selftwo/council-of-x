# The Limits of Intuition

Intuition helps you build strong hypotheses on why something
will or won’t work. And while it is quite useful in creating
hypotheses, you shouldn’t rely on it solely while making
product decisions because

```
Markets change, and you have to continuously update your
mental models to avoid operating on outdated
assumptions. 
We tend to overestimate the impact of our intuition, and
that might lead to flawed confidence. Fundamental
attribution error is quite common here as we tend to
attribute success to our personal/internal factors, whereas
attribute our failures to external factors.
```
And because of these 2 reasons, it’s hard to know the limits of
our intuition. 

An example of this can be seen in the case of Quibi. Veteran
Hollywood executive Jeffrey Katzenberg and former HP CEO
Meg Whitman built Quibi. It goes without saying that they
would have good intuition when it comes to content and tech.
But their intuition failed Quibi in this instance. 

Quibi raised $1.75 billion from investors to create a short-form
streaming platform that generated content for viewing on
mobile devices. 

And while Katzenberg cited the unfortunate timing of the
launch during the pandemic as a contributing factor. That
doesn’t seem a good enough reason for an era where the
entertainment industry was booming because people didn’t
have much beyond digital entertainment during Covid. 

With shut-downs occurring throughout the globe, consumers
relied on digital (video-on-demand, streaming video and
electronic sell through) for entertainment. The trend toward

# Consumer Research

Consumer research is the act of gathering information about
consumers' needs and preferences.

In that context, consumer research helps us in multiple ways:

```
While building 0 to 1 products, it helps us understand the
ground reality of the market by validating/ invalidating the
assumptions we have around our future product. 
In the growth and maturity stage of the product, data can
tell us what the problem is but it can’t tell us why the
problem exists. Research helps us figure the ‘why’ behind
the problem.
The more we understand the users, the better our intuition
becomes. So continuous research helps us build better
intuition.
```
But while consumer research is quite beneficial, and most
product teams know it in some form, many teams don’t do it in
the right way. Given that the outcomes from user research are
highly dependent on the accuracy method, it’s important to
understand how to do it well.

# Recruiting audience

```
Recruiting an audience is generally hard because it is a multi-
stage process and each step needs to be carefully managed:
While reaching out to these customers, we need to set clear
expectations about time commitment and information
needed from them. If there are rewards for participating in
the research, that need to be made clear as well.
The users also have privacy concerns like the research
recordings, or other user personal info getting shared. You
have to ensure these concerns are alleviated.
There needs to be the right scheduling and arrangements
for the research to happen in a smooth manner.
Further, even before recruiting audience, we have to ensure that
we identify the relevant users for the research goal and make
sure to avoid any biased selection:
```
```
Courses
```

```
Identify most relevant users : Before reaching out to
users for research, you should clearly define the criteria for
participants based on the research goals. For example, if
testing a new finance app, you might want users who
regularly use online banking.
Avoid biased selection : There should be representation of
every relevant segment within the users recruited for the
research. For example, a test prep app might find it easy to
recruit top students who are more than eager to
participate, but difficult to recruit the ones who aren’t that
good in studies. The product teams have to avoid a
situation where they end up recruiting mostly top students
as the research would become biased.
```
### Recruitment Process

Once we have defined the criteria, the recruitment can happen
in one or more of the following ways:

```
Existing users : You can reach out to users from your own
database, especially if you want feedback on existing
products or services. Emails, in-app notifications, or direct
messages can be used to get users to fill the information
around their contact, availability and get consent from
them around research. Triggering surveys on particular
actions (intercept surveys) are another way to recruit users.
If you don’t want to do internal development, you can use
tools like Sprig to launch intercept surveys.
Friends and family : Internal employees, friends and
family can help you recruit users if they know people who
fit the profile of research.
Social media and messaging groups : Social media and
messaging groups are a great way to find people for
research. You can also create online social media ads to
recruit such users. 
Referral programs : Already recruited participants can
refer others if there is an incentive to do so. However, you
have to make sure that the referrals fit the profile you are
looking for.
Local community and offline public places : For quick,
informal feedback, you might approach individuals in
public places like coffee shops or parks, ensuring they fit a
broad target profile.

# Avoiding mistakes while

# conducting interviews

There are three key mistakes people make that we want to cover
in this section:

**Over-reliance on a single method** : Triangulation, or using
multiple methods to investigate a research question, can
provide a more comprehensive view and validate findings. A
survey followed by a set of user interviews are quite useful. It’s
even better if you can look into data to find evidence about
what users are asking you to do.

**Stated vs revealed preferences** : What people say (stated)
could be very different than what they actually end up doing
(revealed). This causes most of the problems in user research.
You can avoid it by asking what they do (behaviors rather than
opinions) in most situations. For example, if you are building
an app that helps people cook different recipes, ask how they
find and cook different recipes. Or even better, ask what all food
they cook themselves and check whether cooking different
recipes is even a thing. 

A pretty good book around stated vs revealed preference is The
Mom Test. The central thesis of the book is that most people
who know you, including your mom, don’t want to hurt your
feelings. So when you're trying to validate a business idea or
gather feedback on a product, you should ask questions in a way
that even your mom couldn't lie to you. In other words, your
questions should be unbiased, open-ended, and focused on past
behaviors rather than hypothetical situations. A question that
gets the honest response from your mom passes the mom test.

## Leading questions

```
You should avoid asking leading questions in a research
interview. And while most of the people who have been through
```
```
Courses
```

the research know it, it’s very hard to resist asking a leading
question around a product we are so vested in. Here are few
rules to follow so that you can avoid leading question:

**Do not rephrase in your own words**
_Participant: “I noticed the picture here..”
Researcher: “You mentioned that the picture was helpful. What did
you like about it?”_
Problem: Researcher is rephrasing what user said and
asking leading question – what do you like..?  
Improvement: “You notice the picture here. Could you talk
more about it?”

**Do not suggest an answer**
_“How well would this save time for you in a week?” _
Problem: The question leads the user to think that the product
saves time. 
Improvement: “How will this affect your productivity?”

**Do not name/label an element on the screen**
_“The related links on the bottom of the page. Can you think where
they will lead?”_
Problem: Your mentioning of the term ‘related links’ can build
associations in the user's mind that would not happen normally
in a real-world scenario.
Improvement: “The area at the bottom of the page.. What is
that?”

**Do not assume and mention what user is feeling**
_“When you were struggling with this task, what were you
thinking?”_
Problem: You have assumed the user was struggling with the
task. It makes them conscious and biased. In their response,
they are going to focus on talking about struggles.
Improvement: “What was easy or difficult about the task you
just did?”

**To summarize, your goal is to minimize the bias you can
introduce in the research process.**

# Summary

```
Product marketing has gained significance with
platforms like Airbnb shifting Product Managers (PMs)
to more marketing-focused roles. However that isn't a
surprise. Historically, the PM role encompassed what is
currently considered product marketing, with PMs
overseeing the entire product lifecycle, from research to
monetization. The tasks of acquisition and
monetization, essential parts of the growth cycle,
overlap with product marketing roles. Ben Horowitz's
'Good Product Manager, Bad Product Manager'
emphasized this overlap, suggesting the software PM
role included the Product Marketing Manager (PMM).
However, with time, PMs began focusing more on
program management, overshadowing their core duties
of consumer insight, value creation, and monetization.
Yet, current market challenges demand PMs to focus on
growth metrics, necessitating skills in positioning,
monetization, research, and competitive strategy.
It's essential to recognize the limitations of intuition in
product decisions. Reliance solely on intuition can lead
to significant market miscalculations, as observed with
the failure of Quibi. To overcome these limits, several
methods like Clickstream data and user research can
provide crucial insights.
Consumer research plays a vital role in understanding
consumers' needs and preferences, validating product
assumptions, addressing issues, and refining product
strategy. Effective research requires defining clear goals,
selecting appropriate methodologies, recruiting the right
audience, and avoiding common pitfalls such as leading
questions and over-reliance on a single method.
