# Introduction

Product can play an important role in acquisition, no matter
which area it is. However, the involvement of PMs varies
depending on the channels. Here is a simple framework to
decide when you should lead vs assist.

```
Lead in areas where you have high context and good
control on all inputs. 
Assist in areas where you have limited context, and limited
control on inputs
```
Let’s try to apply this to a couple of channels we have discussed
so far.

```
Paid marketing: PMs have limited context and limited
control. They can’t get a say in the budget but merely
evaluate and understand them. So you should assist here.
Product referrals: The incentives in product referrals is
part of the product experience, and the product has way
better context and control on such referrals. You can build
more value for the referrer and hence improve the
conversion. On the other hand, if the referral being
disbursed is cash, there isn’t much a product team can do
beyond creating a usable flow. The budget for cash referrals
is usually controlled by the marketing team.
```

## Tracking and attribution

```
To gauge performance of digital channels, product teams help
in event tracking. Putting the right tracking and
instrumentation helps in attributing users to the right
channels.
In absence of such measures, the mismeasurement happens.
And what gets mismeasured, gets mismanaged.
While you are at it, one of the key challenges you will find in
marketing and sales organizations of all shapes and sizes is
attribution models. A user doesn’t get converted the first time
they arrive to the product for most products out there. That
means, the same user can come via different channels. At one
```
```
Courses
```

time, they click on a FB ad. At another, they click on a google
ad. 

Attribution models focus on figuring out which channels are
most effective in converting the users. Here are a few key
attribution models 

```
First Click: 100% of credit goes to the channel that user
came first from.
Last Click: 100% of credit goes to the channel that user
came last from.
Linear: Credit is split among every touch
Position Based: Few positions like first and last touch get
higher credit than the rest
Time Decay: As the time passes, the credit attributed to a
channel goes down. Heavily weighted towards the last
touch.
```
Depending on the product you have at hand, one attribution
model may make more sense than another. Let’s solve a couple
of problems to understand it better.

# Referrals

```
Referral is getting your existing users to promote your product
and bring new users.  The reason referral works so well is
because when someone we know or trust tells us about a
product, we are more inclined to try it. And if the virtuous cycle
continues, it keeps bringing more and more users to the
product, resulting in explosive growth.
```
```
We can categorize referrals in two types:
Incentivised referrals : In incentivised referrals, you
incentivise one side or both sides when they get a new user
onboarded. The incentive can be money, or another benefit
like premium features. 
For example, Dropbox offered free storage for referrals, whereas
Uber offered gift cards that could be used to pay for rides. 
Let’s look at how incentivised referrals work. In order to track
which user has referred a new user, the referrer usually shares a
unique referral code/link that the product generates for them.
This unique referral code helps the product in tracking where
```
```
Courses
```

the new users came from, so that it can disburse incentives
accordingly. Below is an image of the Uber referral screen.

In one of the best examples of referrals, a game like Candy
Crush got really popular by asking players to invite their friends
in order to get lives and to play more.

```
Organic referrals : Organic means no incentives on either
side for the referral. But why would someone promote your
product on their own? The motivations in this case are
often intrinsic like goodwill, social proof, and prestige etc.
The mode of organic referral can be both digital (shared on
```
```
Courses
social media and social groups), and offline/word of mouth
(shared on phone, dinner, parties). Let’s discuss how shares
on social media platforms and groups happen. 
Organic shares of social platforms happen when people love the
content so much, they love promoting it. The only word of
caution is that while going viral is most rewarding in terms of
ROI, it’s also the hardest to come up with a viral video or
content. 
An example of a product that grew on top of organic social
shares are Dollar Shaving Club whose video got viral on social
media garnering 10s of millions of views, attracting a large
customer base. On July 19, 2016, Dollar Shave Club was
acquired by Unilever for a reported $1 billion in cash. 
```
```
While building a piece of content that people keep sharing is
hard, another form of organic referral comes through network
effect. Since the experience of a user usually improves by
onboarding their friends, family, coworkers, etc., users are
intrinsically motivated to share the product and onboard their
close groups. 
Many B2C products such as Whatsapp, Telegram, Facebook, etc.
are examples of this. Many B2C products like Notion, Slack, etc.
have also benefited from organic referrals since they have
inherent network effects.
```

# Common Mistakes in Growth

# Modeling

While creating a growth model, you may come across outliers. A
recent outlier which is valid for a lot of companies is Covid. 

The user behavior changed significantly during Covid for
entertainment and eCommerce apps. People spent a lot of time
at home and couldn’t go out. So they kept themselves busy with
entertainment apps. Because they couldn’t go out, all the
offline transactions shifted online.

As we are back to normal now in 2024, the retention and
acquisition data now is quite different from the Covid era.
That’s one example of outlier data which we can’t use, or have
to use with caution.

```
Courses
The example also brings the most important aspect of an
outlier data to avoid – using data from a different environment.
The environment of Covid era is quite different from post-Covid
era.
```
```
Another example of such data is festive sales on eCommerce
apps in India. The new customers and their retention is quite
different during festive sales from non-festive days. So we have
to make sure that we don’t use festive data to make predictions
above business as usual.
The next mistake is to use stale data. In the examples, we
covered in this section, we used an average of recent month
retention (t-12 months) for future projections. In case the
product is old and has data for a longer time period, say 5 years,
we don’t need to take an average of 5 years because the
historical data beyond a particular period won’t be relevant for
the growth model. The market, competition, and user behavior
changes over the years, and so does acquisition and retention. 
A subset of stale data is using data from a different stage of the
product. For example, in the early stage (first 3-6 months),
products grow via early adopters like friends, family, etc.  First
100 users would display different kinds of loyalty than when we
scale to have 10 k MAU. Be sure to not use data from the early
stage to predict growth stage numbers.
We also have to account for seasonality for products where
seasonality affects usage. An example of this is tax preparation
software. We can’t average out in such cases, and have to be
careful.
```
```
To summarize, keep the following in mind
Be mindful of using data that has a different environment
or context.
Remove stale data – beyond a particular time period, and
from a different stage of the product.
Do not take anomalies into the growth model.
Take seasonality into account.
```

# Accuracy in Growth Models

The answer to the accuracy question is subjective and depends
on your goal as a product team.

For mature products, you can pick a goal to be more accurate in
your projections. Mature products have years of past data to
look upon, and hence the expectation from stakeholders is that
your projections would be accurate. In such cases, you can even
take into account the variation due to certain events or
seasonality. For example, the black Friday sales in the US, or
Festival sales in India are a regular event, and can be accounted
for in the growth model. Years of data also allows you to remove
any variation that is an outlier. So in this case, we can be more
accurate.

Accuracy is also a big concern in public companies, where not
meeting the quarterly projections can hurt the stock. So we
should aspire to predict well in such companies.

But if the company is at an early stage and is trying to figure
out the key variables that influence growth, then the bar for
accuracy will be lower. You need to be directionally accurate
and not far off from actual numbers. 

So how do you make models more accurate?

To get a more accurate model, rather than using the average of
the last N months, we need to calculate how the retention and
acquisition has changed over time. This gives us a good
baseline of improvements in future. 

We can then look at our future roadmap and estimate how
much we would be able to affect retention and acquisition. This
would allow us to predict the growth accurately. 

A detailed growth is also more accurate. So we also need to
create the model for different channels and sum them up
because it will be more accurate given that users from a
channel behave consistently, and we can take into account any
changes in the spend.

No matter where you are, I would recommend you to start with
a simpler model and then make it progressively complex by
adding channels and then improvements. That will help you
understand what variables affect growth better than directly
creating a complex model.

# Limitations of Growth Models

```
When building the quantitative model, we used the historical
data. Therefore, the model makes forecasts by assuming that
the future will be like the past.
But in real life, the market and competition can change the
landscape rapidly, and the forecast model will not be able to
take these changes into account. For example, Blockbuster's
growth model did not account for the emergence of Netflix’s
more efficient solution and how it would affect key metrics and
overall company growth.
But despite the fact that the default model does not take into
account future changes, such a model gives you all the tools to
figure out the current growth problems and anticipate problems
as you update this model over time. If you can figure out the
problems early, you can also try to find the root cause and solve
them. 
You can start on the journey of growth modeling by creating
more realistic growth targets. The growth model can give you a
reality check early on, and push the team to find more projects
that can actually help you meet targets.
```

# Quantitative Growth Model

## Introduction

No matter where you work, be it a startup or a big company,
people will expect you to set growth targets if you are leading a
product or business.

We often take an audacious growth target that looks good to
others. A hypothetical growth target discussion looks like this.

_“It’s Sept 2020 and the product has 1 million daily active users
(DAU). The company wants to achieve 2 million DAU by Mar 2021.

# Summary

```
Growth modeling starts by defining the NSM. We can use
the following criteria to define NSM:
It should represent the value users receive
It should be a leading indicator of value for the
business
It can be measured accurately through the product
It should be sensitive to product changes
It should have a clear, practical meaning
It should be hard to game
Once we have defined the NSM, we can move on to the
next step – identifying growth levers by creating a
growth levers diagram or GLD.
A growth lever diagram relies upon the growth equation
to break down NSM and lists an exhaustive set of levers
that ultimately affect NSM. What’s better is that the GLD
can also be used to create a root–cause analysis for
growth problems such as a dip in the metric.
```

# Identifying Ceiling of Growth

To identify the ceiling of growth, we need to get the following:

```
Market size (ARPU * N, where N is the number of
customers in your market)
% of market you can capture (P)
```
Your revenue ceiling would be a function of these three
numbers, i.e.

**_Revenue ceiling = (ARPU * N) * P_**

Let’s discuss how to calculate the three numbers one-by-one.

# ARPU

ARPU is the most straightforward of the three if you are already
monetising the product. You can look at the current ARPU and
use that number. 

If you have a pre-revenue product, you need to conduct
research around customer willingness to pay (WTP). Once you
have the WTP, you can use the same as the proxy of ARPU. We
will cover how to find WTP in detail in the monetisation

# Potential Target Market

So does it mean that vision and long-term strategy for
expanding your target market isn’t useful? They are useful
actually. They help us determine the direction of a company's
development. They also helps us see strategic areas of growth
where the company should invest in new initiatives. 

Going from 0 to 1 (bringing a new product to market, finding
product/market fit, finding channels) and going from 1 to N
(scaling a product that has proven to be in demand) require
different approaches, processes, skills and people.

In a confirmed target market, the team has already figured the
0-to-1 journey, and should scale (1 to N) by bringing the
product’s value to as many of the target audience as possible.
The team will also work on improving the strength of PMF. 

In a potential target market, the team first needs to create value
and achieve product/market fit. Only after that can it switch to
scaling mode. Going from 0 to 1 is a risky venture and that’s
why it shouldn’t be included in growth projections before
hitting PMF. 

As soon as you can prove that the product has PMF outside of
the initially confirmed target market, the estimate for market
size can be changed.

# Final Word

Estimating market size can be complicated because new
companies can change the size of the market (TAM or Total
Addressable Market) by creating a more efficient way to solve
the problem. New technologies or new business models can
increase efficiency or reduce the cost for end users. 

For example, at one time, the target market for printers was just
business customers. But the launch of new, more affordable
products led to an expansion of the market to the B2C
segment. 

The concept of market expansion was popularized by Bill
Gurley in his essay “All Markets Are Not Created Equal: 10
Factors To Consider When Evaluating Digital Marketplaces”.
Gurley also wrote a famous essay in response to criticism of
Uber for inflating the company's value. The criticism boiled
down to the fact that Uber is greatly overvalued due to the
restrictions on the size of the taxi market. In the essay “How to
Miss By a Mile: An Alternative Look at Uber’s Potential Market
Size,” Gurley shared his thoughts on how Uber is actually
expanding the taxi market by creating a more efficient solution
to the problem of moving from point A to point B.

This brings us back to the important conclusion we made in the
last chapter: The market should not be determined based on
artificial categories (for example, taxi), but on the problem that
a person wants to solve (get from one place to another). In this
case, the list of competitors expands to all alternative methods
for solving the problem and even situations where people have
completely abandoned the problem due to the lack of a suitable
solution.

As it turns out, Uber has expanded the taxi market in many

# Quantifying product-channel

# fit

To quantify product-channel fit, we have to understand CAC
and LTV. CAC is short for customer acquisition cost, which is
money spent on acquiring a single customer from a specific
channel. 

LTV is short for lifetime value, and denotes the money product
makes on a user acquired in their lifetime. Since the lifetime of
a user varies depending on the product, calculating LTV is
trickier than it seems. 

It goes without saying that the channels with the best ROI
(LTV/CAC) have the strongest impact on overall growth. So the
return on investment or return on ad spend (ROAS) is used to
quantify product-channel fit. 

More on how the equation between CAC and LTV plays an
important role in the next section on monetisation!

# Pre-PMF

If after going through retention curves or customer satisfaction
scores, you realize that your product is pre-PMF, your singular
focus should be attaining PM/F and not growth. 

There are two reasons for focussing on PMF before focussing on
growth:

1. In absence of PMF, you will be continuously spending
    money to acquire customers and losing most of them
    within a short span of time. The value generation and
    monetisation would be lower as a result, and this would
    create a high burn rate for the product.
2. The users who try out your product and don’t get value
    would remember and talk about it. They, along with those
    who hear about it, would think twice before trying your
    product again. All of this would lead to increasing
    customer acquisition costs.

So the question becomes – what do you do to get to product-
market fit? Segment, segment, segment!

# Searching for benchmarks of

To search for benchmarks, we should start at defining the
problem that the product solves well. That helps us refine the
product category. Let’s take a look at dating apps. Different
dating apps serve different purposes. Tinder is an app for casual
dating whereas Hinge is for more serious dating. 

A good way to identify the product category is to look through
the lens of jobs-to-be-done. 

_“People don't buy products; they “hire” them to do jobs, such as
solving a problem or fulfilling a desire – in a particular context.”_

The context and problem are important to define the jobs-to-
be-done. Here are some possible JTBD for Tinder 

1. "When I'm bored and want to meet new people, I want a
    simple way to start casual conversations, so I can be
    entertained and possibly find someone interesting."
2. "When I'm in a new city or area, I want to be able to find
    local people to talk to, so I can explore the social scene and
    not feel alone."
3. "When I'm looking for a casual date, I want to easily see
    who's available, so I can quickly find a match without
    investing too much time or energy."

Here is what it looks like for a serious dating app like
match.com

1. "When I'm ready for a long-term relationship, I want a
    service that helps me find people who are compatible on
    deep levels, so I can spend my time talking to potential life
    partners."
2. "When I want to invest in finding love, I need a platform
    that allows me to express who I am in depth, so potential
    matches can understand me better."
3. "When I'm looking for someone who's serious about dating,
    I want a platform that filters out casual browsers, so I can
    focus on meaningful connections.”

# Measuring PMF

There are two ways to measure PMF at scale quantitatively –
user satisfaction and retention. 

Surveys are one way to do it at scale. If a user is satisfied with a
product, it will reflect in satisfaction surveys. The metric for
satisfaction is customer satisfaction score where you ask the
customer to rate their experience on a scale of 1 to 5. As an
example, let’s assume that the distribution of users rating their

The overall CSAT score is calculated by SUMPRODUCT of
Rating and % - 5*0.66 + 4*0.14 + 3*0.1 + 2*0.08 + 1*0.02 = 4.34

If you have a product and want to benchmark your CSAT score,
anything above 4 is considered good. You can also Google for
average CSAT scores in your industry. Various research
organizations publish such benchmarks. 

Satisfaction score is a leading metric for product-market fit.
The issue, however, is not all customers rate their experience.
So we don't have the full picture. So what do we do to get the
full picture?

Let’s take a step back and think. Since a product with strong
PMF will have a high % of them satisfied with it, these users
will keep coming back again to the product. This means that
the product would have high retention. 
And so customer retention is a strong indicator of PMF.
Further, it can account for all the product users.

# Measuring PMF for Infrequent

# Products

Retention as a measure of PMF can’t be applied to products
with infrequent usage. To keep things simple, we will classify
the products with repeats happening > 6 months as infrequent
products.

One can argue that even for infrequent products, retention is a
good metric to look at. For example, it will be useful to look at
annual retention for a tax website, even if the users visit it
infrequently, i.e. once or twice a year. By tracking retention, you
can know whether the website is able to retain end-users for a
multi-year period. 

The problem with relying on retention in such cases is that as
the time-window gets bigger (years in the case of a tax website),
it becomes harder to create a fast feedback loop to measure
PMF. We would have to ship features and wait for a year to get
feedback on PMF. Retention in such cases becomes a lagging
metric of PMF. For this reason, we have to rely on CSAT.

# Summary

Before investing in scale, we need to determine if the
product is ready for growth by looking at: 
product-market fit (PMF), 
availability of scalable channels, 
product monetization, and 
market saturation

Of these factors, PMF is a necessary criteria for growth.
It also is the hardest one among the four. 

**Cycle of Growth** : The cycle of growth is defined as 
Acquisition
Value generation
Monetisation

**Frequency** : Products vary in how frequently they deliver
value. For instance, Amazon provides consistent value by
offering a wide selection at good prices, leading to
repeated purchases. In contrast, platforms like Zillow
generate value infrequently as people don't buy homes
regularly.

**Value generation** : Value gets generated in many ways

```
Attention products: Offer entertainment, content,
and social connections. Value is measured by time
spent and active users. Examples include YouTube
and Facebook.
Transaction products: Help in transactions and
purchases. Value is quantified by the number of
transactions and revenue. Examples include
Amazon and Airbnb.
Productivity products: Make tasks easier and save
time. Value is determined by successful task
completions and time saved. Examples are Slack and
Whatsapp.
```
**PMF** : Value generation isn’t sufficient for PMF. A
product must be preferred over others even if it delivers
value. PMF is having a product favored over others
addressing the same issue. PMF can be measured via

```
User satisfaction: Can be determined through
surveys, where a Customer Satisfaction Score (CSAT)
is calculated. A score above 4 is generally considered
good.
```
```

PMF. Retention is assessed based on how many new
users return to the product after a given period.
Measuring retention : It is the percentage of new users
retained after a specific duration. Retention frequency
(daily, monthly, etc.) depends on how frequently a
product is naturally used. For example, music apps have
daily retention while platforms like Airbnb might be
checked semi-annually.
Retention directly influences product growth. A product
with a flattened retention curve indicates PMF, while a
declining one implies a lack of PMF.

relying on retention can be challenging. In such cases,
```

# Moats In the Growth Stage

In the growth stage, three additional advantages can appear

```
Courses
Economies of scale: Economies of scale implies that the cost
to serve a user reduces with scale. 
Suppose a printer costs $900 and lasts 30 days. The cost of
printing one page (ink and paper) is $1. Now assume that we
print 10 pages daily to serve 10 users, so a printer in 30 days
prints 300 pages. Cost of printing per page would be
($300+$900)/ 300 = $4 per page.
Now, imagine that we increased the scale and attract 100 users,
i.e. number of pages we are printing per day to 100. Total
number of pages printed in 30 days would be 3000. Cost of
printing per page would be ($3000+$900) /3000 = $1.3.
So as we increase the scale, the cost per page goes down. It
subsequently brings the cost to serve every user. This is known
as economies of scale.
Network effects: Network effects mean that the value of
the product increases as you add more users. For example,
as more of your family and friends onboard on a messaging
app such as Whatsapp, the value you get out of the app
increases. 
```
```
Switching cost: Switching cost is another advantage in
the growth stage. 
Think of sellers on an eCommerce site like eBay or Amazon who
accumulate ratings over time as more customers buy from
them. Because they have reviews and ratings on the platform,
they can’t move out of it because they will end up losing the
reputation they have built over the years on the platform. 
So switching the product has a cost for these sellers (also
known as switching cost), and the higher the cost is, the more
prone they are to stick with the product. 
```

# Model

The last two steps of holistic view are distribution and business
model. We won’t be covering them extensively here because

Distribution means how the product is going to acquire its
customers. The early customers can come via cold reach outs,
friends, peers, etc. But getting early customers through these
channels doesn’t mean we have figured out the distribution of
the product. 

What you need to find actually are scalable channels, i.e.
channels that can keep providing you new users if you put more
effort and money into that channel. So for a new product, you
need to figure out at least 1 or 2 channels that are going to be
scalable.

For existing products, you should understand which channels
work currently and why. That will help you understand the
distribution strategy of the existing product.

We will discuss scalable channels in module 5 around
acquisition.

When it comes to business models, people focus on a single
metric like revenue, or GMV, or users in the early days. While
having focus on a single metric is good in the early days so that
you can put all your energies behind it, it is not advisable in the
long run. 

In the long run, we should focus on a holistic business model
that can lead to profits. This means looking at all the
components of business such as the costs, pricing, profitability,
etc.

large market that was promised to raise funds.

preferences. For our toothbrush example, a metro user might be
comfortable with buying a premium toothbrush whereas a tier-

adult. In this way, we can see that different segments would
have different needs and preferences. Capturing a large market
means solving for various segment needs and preferences,
better than the alternatives.

# Defining 'Growth' for PMs

Growth is a subjective term which is used to describe different
things by different teams. When a marketing team is talking
about growth, they usually mean growing users by spending
marketing dollars. When a sales team talks about it, they mean
growing revenue by setting up calls/meetings to convert leads
into paying customers.

So what does it mean for product teams? Product teams are
usually focussed on building software so that they can affect
the north star of the company, like users, revenue, or
engagement. Growing the north star is possibly the best thing a
team can do, and PMs can contribute a lot in doing so. Software
is eating the world, and that way they have the best lever –
Software. 

Product-led Growth is a term that has gotten popular in PM
literature recently. Its popularity signifies that a software
product can be a strong enabler to growth, or at the very least
people want a software product team to contribute to growth.
That is what we are going to cover in this course.

But can you apply product-led growth (PLG) to companies of all
shapes and sizes?

# Where is PLG Effective?

```
The PLG is effective in two ways:
First, it can reduce the amount of money spent on acquiring a
new customer, also known as customer acquisition cost (CAC).
PLG’s impact is proportional to how much it can reduce the
CAC. 
Second, the revenue growth in companies also comes from
converting new customers and retaining the existing
customers. New customers can convert quickly after seeing the
value in the product. And if you retain customers, the life time
value of the customer increases, thereby increasing overall
revenue. So conversion and retention are key responsibilities of
a growth PM. 
```
