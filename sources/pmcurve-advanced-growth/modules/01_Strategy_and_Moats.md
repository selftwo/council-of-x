# Introduction to Strategy

To understand strategy, we need to first understand why we
need strategy. And to answer that, we have to start with the
principle of competitive exclusion. 

In 1934, Professor Gause of Moscow University published the
results of a set of experiments in which he put two very small
animals (protozoans) of the same genus in a bottle. The
conditions were to add fresh water every day and input a
constant flow of food. If the animals were of different species,
they could survive and persist together. If they were of the same
species, they could not. Gause called it the Principle of
Competitive Exclusion: two species that make their living in an
identical manner cannot coexist indefinitely. The reason being -
they compete for the same resources and hence survival of only
one of them is possible in the long run.

Products follow the law of competitive exclusion. The two
products solving the same problem for the same customer
base can’t coexist indefinitely. Competitors perpetually crowd
each other out.

This principle explains why strategy is important – strategy is
about choosing what to be, and how it can create an advantage
over competitors.

# Building the advantage

# through choices

The competitive advantage is all about the difference in the
ways a company does activities like design, produce, sell,
market, service, etc., across the value chain. These differences
determine the relative cost and pricing as compared to the
competition. 

Various activities should **fit across the chain** to ensure it

and hardware for Apple are tightly integrated. It becomes more
distinctive when they offer a sophisticated sales force, premium
assistance, and support in Apple Stores. It will be very hard to
copy Apple end-to-end since one will have to copy the activities
across the board. 

The second thing to build advantage is to have a stable strategy
over time. Changing strategy frequently can confuse employees
and consumers both. Continuing with the current strategy for a
reasonable amount of time provides ample time and
opportunity for the company to evolve itself without throwing
everything in chaos.

# Moats

The term refers to a business's ability to maintain competitive
advantages over its competitors in order to protect its long-
term profits and market share. The term comes as an analogy
from a medieval castle, where the moat serves to protect those
inside the fortress and their riches from outsiders.

Let’s take a simple example to understand it. Suppose you have
decided to make your fortune by running a lemonade stand. You
realize that if you buy your lemons in bulk once a week instead
of every morning, you can reduce your expenses by 30%,
allowing you to undercut the prices of competing lemonade
stands.

Your low prices lead to an increase in the number of customers
buying lemonade from you (and not from your competitors).

As a result, you see an increase in profits; however, it probably
wouldn't take very long for your competitors to notice your
method and employ it themselves. Therefore, in a short period
of time, your large profits would erode, and the local lemonade
industry would return to normal conditions again.
However, suppose you develop and patent a juicing technology
that allows you to get 30% more juice out of the average lemon.
This would have the same effect of reducing your average cost
per glass of lemonade. This time, your competitors will have no
way of duplicating your methods, as your competitive
advantage is protected by your patent. That’s the example of a
moat.

# Aligning everyone

Aligning everyone to create a coherent set of choices is usually
difficult. Across the companies, these two tools can be used to
create a coherent set of choices: 

**Objectives and Key Results (OKRs)** : OKRs act as a bridge
between high-level strategy and day-to-day operations. Here's
how the process looks:

1. **Define top objectives** : Start with the organization's
    overarching strategy. This could be a long-term vision or a
    strategic focus for the year. Translate this vision into a set
    of top-level objectives. For example, an objective might be
    "Become the leading brand in the mid-tier smartphone
    market."
2. **Identify key results** : For each objective, define 2-5 key
    results. Key results are specific, time-bound, and
    measurable outcomes. Using the earlier example, a key
    result might be "Achieve a 25% market share in the mid-
    tier smartphone segment by Q4."
3. **Cascade OKRs** : Break down organizational OKRs into
    department-specific or team-specific OKRs. This helps
    each department understand their role and contribution

# Prioritizing the Initiatives

```
The most popular method for prioritization is RICE, which is an
abbreviation for reach, impact, confidence, and effort. 
Reach is a measure of how many users will be affected by a
feature.
Impact is a measure of how much of a difference the
feature will make for users.
Confidence is a measure of how confident the product team
is that the feature will be successful.
Effort is a measure of how much effort will be required to
develop the feature.
```
```
Courses
```

**RICE** is a pretty good way of prioritization among initiatives,
and while there are few other methods of prioritization, every
method comes down to effort, overall impact, and how
confident we are. 

A common struggle with RICE is that teams often struggle to
put the confidence score of a feature. Assigning a score to
Confidence can be very tricky because it’s by far the most
subjective and intangible dimension, which makes it
susceptible to bias and gut feel. That can lead to conflict among
teams. So instead of assessing how confident you are, it’s better
to establish your Confidence score through data.

A good starting point would be to collect qualitative feedback
from your customers, quantitative data, and experiments that
you have conducted in the past. Even if your product is at its
early stages, customer interviews can significantly improve
your decision-making during prioritization. But customers
aren’t the only place to look. You can look to collect various
other types of evidence:

1. A/B tests conducted in the past can inform decisions in the
    future. 
2. Cohort analysis also helps in establishing strong
    correlations and should be used as such. For example, two
    groups of users joining at the same time can be compared
    and we can try to find what led to different behavior among
    the two segments. 
3. Finding segments which show success and failure, and
    compare what led to success or failure to find correlations. 
4. We can rely on competitor case studies, or case studies
    from similar domains
5. We can reply on expert opinions

```

Usually, the quality of evidence isn’t the same across methods.
User interviews trump everything if done well, and a/b tests >
cohort analysis > segmentation analysis > case studies > expert
opinions.
```

# Monetisation

Monetisation is the third stage of growth and is super
important. So far, we have looked at acquisition and value
generation. Monetisation is about generating revenue so that
you can keep spending money on growth, i.e. acquiring new
users, and generating value for them.

# Monetisation works via

# incentive to buy and sell

You make revenue by setting a price for the product. Your gross
profit is the difference between price and variable costs. So
many companies do a cost-based pricing where they look at all
the costs, add a margin and then set the price. After you want to
make gross profit as a business, that is your incentive to sell. 

However that isn’t the full picture. As a business, you need to
think about the incentive to buy for the end user. The incentive
to buy is the gap between value generated for the user through
the transaction and price paid for it. The higher the gap, the
higher the incentive to buy. This is why deep discounts work so
well in retail. Limited-time deep discounts increase the
incentive to buy drastically by increasing the gap, and also
introducing a sense of urgency.

# Poor demand

Lack of demand would mean lack of any kind of monetisation.
It usually stems in two ways.

The first one is the products getting built without
understanding why the market would prefer it over alternatives
that would get no demand. Many products built with a mindset
of copying competitors fall under this category. 

An example of this is Microsoft Zune. The company launched
the device in 2006 to compete with the iPod. But the Zune
never got past the single digits in market share.

“ _Microsoft’s player is just as good as an iPod—it performed all of
that device’s main functions pretty well. But there’s no way in
which it’s better than an iPod. And that’s why it was doomed._ ” -
Slate

The second way is when there is demand for the free version of
the product, but not for the paid one. Online news outlets
struggle with this. Many readers expect news to be freely
accessible online. When outlets introduce paywalls for
premium content, they often face resistance from users who
will search for free alternatives instead. So rather than relying
on both ads and user subscription fees like offline newspapers,
online news outlets struggle to make their ends meet through
ads only.

```
product.
Rule #1: It’s better to be first than it is to be better
Regardless of reality, people perceive the first product into the
mind as superior. Marketing is a battle of perceptions, not
products. So unless you can build a 10x better product, or spend
```

**Rule #3: Counter Positioning is quite effective because the
incumbent finds it hard to move against you.**

There are three steps to counter-positioning:
Find a weakness in the competition
Weakness must resonate with consumers
Make this weakness your strength

Netflix versus Blockbuster is a cautionary tale of counter-
positioning. Blockbuster relied on per-rental fees and late
charges for DVDs, which could sometimes frustrate customers.
Customers also had to visit the stores to rent DVDs.

Netflix was built on highlighting the pain points of the
experience—especially late fees and the inconvenience of
physical visits. By offering DVDs by mail and removing late
fees, Netflix was able to erode Blockbuster's market dominance. 

The counter-positioning is effective because the incumbent
can’t offer the same value you are offering due to either
execution risks or business risks. Because the late fees
amounted to $800 million or 16% of Blockbuster's revenues in
2000, the company couldn’t make a move against Netflix. And
that led to their demise in the long run.
