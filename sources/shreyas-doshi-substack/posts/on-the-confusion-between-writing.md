# On the Confusion Between Writing and Thinking

*The idea that “writing is thinking” contains an important truth, but it is routinely overextended and it is not the complete truth. It is useful to understand this if you want to be a clearer thinker.*

Do you know why so many people strongly believe that “writing is thinking”?

It’s because for many **that’s the only time they are doing clear thinking**.

Particularly, it’s the only time they are thinking about _alternative points of view_ (since they know that their writing will be critically evaluated).  
  
Yes, writing does extract some ideas that are otherwise hard to extract. 

And it is reasonable to believe that writing is thinking.  
  
But it is a grave error1 to then conclude that   
a) only writing is thinking, or 

b) writing is the best method for clear thinking for oneself, or 

c) it is not thinking if it is not in writing

Why does this matter in practice?  
It matters because there is an opportunity cost to writing.

And writing has some natural friction for nearly everyone.

So it is quite smart to _also_ develop the habit of thinking clearly without writing.

**Shreyas**

**Claude**

Here are the strongest counterarguments to your post:

**1\. Writing reveals fuzzy thinking that feels clear internally**

The most compelling counter is that people dramatically overestimate the clarity of their non-written thinking. What feels like “clear thinking” in your head often turns out to be vague, contradictory, or poorly structured when you try to write it down. Writing forces precision in a way that mental rehearsal doesn’t - you can’t hand-wave past the hard parts. Many sophisticated thinkers _believe_ they’ve thought something through mentally, but discover gaps only when writing.

**2\. Writing creates artifacts for iteration**

Mental thinking is ephemeral. You can’t easily return to your exact reasoning from yesterday, refine it, or build on it systematically. Writing creates persistent objects you can manipulate, reorganize, and improve. The “opportunity cost” you mention might actually favor writing if it produces compounding returns through iterative refinement that mental thinking can’t achieve.

**3\. The selection bias cuts both ways**

You argue that “writing is thinking” advocates haven’t developed strong mental thinking skills. But the reverse could be true: people who dismiss writing’s cognitive benefits might not have developed sophisticated writing practices. Someone who finds writing high-friction might simply not be very good at it yet. The opportunity cost of writing decreases dramatically with practice.

**4\. Different thinking types require different media**

Some forms of complex, systematic thinking (architectural design of systems, logical proofs, strategic planning with multiple contingencies) may genuinely require external representation. The working memory limitations are real. Your post risks its own fallacy - assuming that because _some_ thinking can be done clearly without writing, _all_ valuable thinking can be.

**5\. You’re conflating “thinking” with “reasoning about counterarguments”**

Your post specifically notes that writing forces consideration of alternative viewpoints because of anticipated critique. But this might not be a bug - it might be precisely what makes writing valuable. If solo mental thinking systematically underweights counterarguments and alternative perspectives, that’s a significant cognitive limitation, not just a stylistic difference.

The strongest version of the counter-case: You’re right that writing shouldn’t be fetishized, but you may be underweighting how much our subjective sense of “clear thinking” misleads us about actual clarity. Writing is less about thinking itself and more about _validating_ that thinking is actually coherent rather than just feeling coherent.

A few things stand out:

**1\. The difference between “when people do X” versus “people can’t do non-X”**

Your statement “for many that’s the only time they are doing real thinking” is describing behavior/habits, not capability limits. **4\. Existence proofs trump theoretical arguments about difficulty**

My arguments about working memory limitations, iterative refinement, etc. all collapse when confronted with: “But accomplished people demonstrably do this.” Theoretical reasons why something should be hard are much weaker than empirical evidence that people actually do it.
