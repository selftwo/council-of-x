# The Product Builder's True Journey

*Product work isn’t just about speed. It’s about 100s of micro-decisions that quietly attach you to what you build, constrain future choices, and determine outcomes before success or failure is visible*

The average product builder lives 2 lives.

In your first life, you just build.

Your second life starts when you finally internalize that you’re going to get attached to & shackled by what you build, no matter what dev process or framework you’re using. You now build more useful things more often and win more often than you did in your first life.

## The Hidden Architecture of Product Failure

#### The Problem with Learning from Success

When exceptional product leaders share their wisdom, they often say things like “just build,” “move fast and iterate,” or “if you’re not embarrassed by v1, you launched too late.” These mantras sound actionable and inspiring. They’re also systematically misleading for most people who hear them.

The exceptional PM, founder, or leader gives this advice in good faith because it genuinely reflects their experience. What they don’t realize—what they can’t realize—is that their success came not from the mantra, but from exceptional judgment that made the mantra safe to follow. When they “just built,” they built the right things. When they “moved fast,” they moved in the right directions. Their attachment to what they created wasn’t costly because what they created was good.

The average builder hears the same advice and applies it with average judgment. They build quickly, but build the wrong things. They iterate, but in unproductive directions. They accumulate features, complexity, and commitments that become increasingly difficult to unwind. And because this advice comes from successful people, they assume they’re doing it right—until the product fails.

This dynamic exists in every human endeavor. The tennis champion says “trust your instincts.” The master investor says “go with your gut.” The acclaimed director says “follow your vision.” In each case, the advice works for the exceptional practitioner because their instincts, gut, and vision are exceptional. For everyone else, the same approach leads to predictable failure.

#### **The Two Lives of Product Work**

The average product builder lives two lives. In the first life, they simply build. They’re energized by creating, by shipping, by seeing their ideas take form. This phase feels productive and purposeful.

The second life begins with a painful realization: you become attached to everything you build, and shackled by it, regardless of your development process or framework. That elegant solution you crafted? You’ll defend it long after it should be killed. That feature you championed? You’ll rationalize its existence even when usage data says otherwise. That architecture you designed? It constrains every subsequent decision, and you’ll resist acknowledging its limitations because doing so means admitting you chose poorly.

This attachment isn’t a personal failing—it’s human nature. Once we invest effort in creating something, our ego becomes intertwined with its survival. The IKEA effect applies to products as powerfully as it does to furniture: we overvalue things we’ve built, not because they’re objectively good, but because we built them.

The shackling is equally inevitable. Every line of code written, every feature shipped, every customer promised a capability becomes a constraint on future decisions. You must maintain it, support it, explain it, and work around it. The costs compound invisibly until you’re spending more effort managing past decisions than making good new ones.

Exceptional builders can defer this reckoning longer because their initial choices are better. The average builder cannot. For them, continuing to “just build” without developing that thoughtful filter is a recipe for accumulating bad attachments and costly shackles.

The discipline of building thoughtfully—building less, building more deliberately, being ruthless about what deserves to exist—isn’t a universal path to success. It’s specifically the compensating strategy for those without exceptional natural judgment.

## The Micro-Decisions That Matter

Here’s what makes this particularly insidious: product work consists of thousands of micro-decisions, made hour by hour, sometimes minute by minute. Should we add this field to the form? Handle this edge case? Refactor now or later? Talk to more customers first? Each requires judgment in context—understanding tradeoffs, predicting consequences, weighing competing values.

The mantras provide zero guidance for these decisions. “Move fast” doesn’t tell you whether to add the field. “Fail fast” doesn’t reveal which edge case matters. These are outcome-level philosophies, not decision-level tools.

Consider a team building a B2B analytics dashboard. An exceptional PM might intuitively know: add the export button now but defer custom date ranges; this edge case matters but that one doesn’t; this complexity will pay off but that complexity won’t. They “just build” and make fifty good micro-decisions per day.

An average PM on the same team, following the same “just build” philosophy, makes fifty mediocre decisions per day. They add features that seem useful but create maintenance burden. They skip conversations that would have revealed the real problem. They optimize for easy execution rather than right execution. Each individual decision seems defensible, but the aggregate creates a product that’s simultaneously over-built and under-serving.

## The Seven Symptoms

Five years ago, I documented seven biases and fallacies that cause smart product teams to build products with mediocre impact:

**The Execution Orientation Fallacy:** We make decisions based on what we can easily execute today rather than what we should build. A team keeps iterating on their consumer mobile app because they have strong mobile engineers, even though the real opportunity is in desktop workflows for professionals.

**The Bias-for-Building Fallacy:** We don’t thoroughly understand the problem because we feel compelled to be in constant motion. A fintech startup builds payment features for three months before discovering their target customers already have payment solutions they like—the real pain point was reporting.

**The IKEA Effect for Products:** We base current decisions on past execution because we’re attached to work already done. A product team maintains a complex admin panel that three customers use because they spent six months building it, instead of deprecating it and focusing on the core product.

**The Focusing Illusion:** We overestimate problem severity when talking to customers about it. Every customer interview about slow page load times makes it feel critical, but when you ship the optimization, usage barely budges because the real friction was elsewhere.

**Maslow’s Hammer:** We overuse familiar frameworks even when inappropriate. A growth team runs A/B tests on everything including brand positioning and customer support tone, because metrics-driven optimization is their hammer.

**The Authority Approval Bias:** We conceive features based on what will get approval rather than what customers need. A PM pitches an AI feature because the CEO mentioned AI in the last all-hands, not because customer research suggests it would help.

**Russian Roulette for Products:** We don’t account for catastrophic scenarios. A social app ships viral sharing mechanics without considering harassment vectors, leading to a PR crisis six months later.

At the time, I suggested the solution was awareness and culture change: name these biases, create shared vocabulary, have leaders call them out upward and downward.

That solution now feels insufficient. These aren’t separate problems to be solved through better process. They’re symptoms of the deeper dynamic: average builders get attached to what they build and shackled by it. You can’t eliminate these biases through awareness any more than you can eliminate gravity through awareness.

## Why Failures Are Misdiagnosed

When products fail, teams conduct postmortems. The conclusions are remarkably consistent: insufficient resources, poor market timing, inadequate engineering velocity, lack of executive support, unexpected competition, shifting strategy, missing data.

Notice what’s never blamed: thinking and judgment.

Yet every failure is ultimately a failure of thinking. Insufficient resources? Someone made poor prioritization and scoping decisions. Bad market timing? Someone misjudged market readiness. Slow engineering? Someone chose the wrong technical approach or feature set. No exec support? Someone failed to think strategically about stakeholder management.

Failures are attributed to circumstances and execution rather than thinking for predictable reasons:

It’s personally threatening to admit “we thought poorly”—it implicates everyone’s competence, especially leadership. Much safer to blame circumstances beyond our control.

Thinking is invisible. You can point to “only two engineers on the team” but you can’t point to “our collective judgment about customer needs was mediocre.”

We lack vocabulary for thinking failures. Most teams have no language for diagnosing where and how their thinking went wrong.

Organizational incentives reward leaders who “did everything right” despite failure—showed good process, worked hard, followed best practices. Admitting judgment failure offers no such protection.

The result: teams perpetually misdiagnose failures, extract wrong lessons, and repeat the same thinking errors. They hire more engineers, adjust timelines, improve their roadmapping process—and continue making the same poor micro-decisions that doom products.

## The Path Forward

The uncomfortable truth is that most product builders are average, not exceptional. This isn’t a moral judgment—it’s statistical reality. And for the average builder, the path to winning is not emulating exceptional builders’ approaches, but recognizing the constraints of average judgment and compensating accordingly.

This means building less. Building more deliberately. Being ruthless about what deserves to exist. Accepting that you’ll get attached to whatever you create and shackled by it, and therefore choosing very carefully what to create in the first place.

It means recognizing that frameworks and mantras won’t transform your judgment from average to exceptional. The actual work—those thousands of micro-decisions—still requires judgment that can’t be shortcut.

And it means being honest in postmortems: most failures are failures of thinking, even when that’s uncomfortable to admit. The team that can name “we failed to recognize our attachment to the original vision was clouding our judgment about pivot timing” is far more likely to improve than the team that blames “insufficient runway.”

This applies not just to product work but to every domain of human endeavor. The pattern is universal because the underlying reality is universal: exceptional talent exists, average talent is far more common, and the advice from the exceptional misleads the average.

The question is whether we’re willing to see it clearly.

## **The Hidden Architecture of Product Failure**

#### **The Problem with Learning from Success**

When exceptional product leaders share their wisdom, they often say things like “just build,” “move fast and iterate,” or “if you’re not embarrassed by v1, you launched too late.” These mantras sound actionable and inspiring. They’re also systematically misleading for most people who hear them.

The exceptional PM, founder, or leader gives this advice in good faith because it genuinely reflects their experience. What they don’t realize—what they can’t realize—is that their success came not from the mantra, but from exceptional judgment that made the mantra safe to follow. When they “just built,” they built the right things. When they “moved fast,” they moved in the right directions. Their attachment to what they created wasn’t costly because what they created was good.

The average builder hears the same advice and applies it with average judgment. They build quickly, but build the wrong things. They iterate, but in unproductive directions. They accumulate features, complexity, and commitments that become increasingly difficult to unwind. And because this advice comes from successful people, they assume they’re doing it right—until the product fails.

This dynamic exists in every human endeavor. The tennis champion says “trust your instincts.” The master investor says “go with your gut.” The acclaimed director says “follow your vision.” In each case, the advice works for the exceptional practitioner because their instincts, gut, and vision are exceptional. For everyone else, the same approach leads to predictable failure.

#### **The Two Lives of Product Work**

The average product builder lives two lives. In the first life, they simply build. They’re energized by creating, by shipping, by seeing their ideas take form. This phase feels productive and purposeful.

The second life begins with a painful realization: you become attached to everything you build, and shackled by it, regardless of your development process or framework. That elegant solution you crafted? You’ll defend it long after it should be killed. That feature you championed? You’ll rationalize its existence even when usage data says otherwise. That architecture you designed? It constrains every subsequent decision, and you’ll resist acknowledging its limitations because doing so means admitting you chose poorly.

This attachment isn’t a personal failing—it’s human nature. Once we invest effort in creating something, our ego becomes intertwined with its survival. The IKEA effect applies to products as powerfully as it does to furniture: we overvalue things we’ve built, not because they’re objectively good, but because we built them.

The shackling is equally inevitable. Every line of code written, every feature shipped, every customer promised a capability becomes a constraint on future decisions. You must maintain it, support it, explain it, and work around it. The costs compound invisibly until you’re spending more effort managing past decisions than making good new ones.

Exceptional builders can defer this reckoning longer because their initial choices are better. The average builder cannot. For them, the question becomes: what do you do once you recognize these forces are real and inescapable?

#### **The Micro-Decisions That Matter**

Here’s what makes this particularly insidious: product work consists of thousands of micro-decisions, made hour by hour, sometimes minute by minute. Should we add this field to the form? Handle this edge case? Refactor now or later? Talk to more customers first? Each requires judgment in context—understanding tradeoffs, predicting consequences, weighing competing values.

The mantras provide zero guidance for these decisions. “Move fast” doesn’t tell you whether to add the field. “Fail fast” doesn’t reveal which edge case matters. These are outcome-level philosophies, not decision-level tools.

Consider a team building a B2B analytics dashboard. An exceptional PM might intuitively know: add the export button now but defer custom date ranges; this edge case matters but that one doesn’t; this complexity will pay off but that complexity won’t. They “just build” and make fifty good micro-decisions per day.

An average PM on the same team, following the same “just build” philosophy, makes fifty mediocre decisions per day. They add features that seem useful but create maintenance burden. They skip conversations that would have revealed the real problem. They optimize for easy execution rather than right execution. Each individual decision seems defensible, but the aggregate creates a product that’s simultaneously over-built and under-serving.

The gap between exceptional and average isn’t visible in the philosophy—both say “just build.” It’s only visible in the accumulated results of thousands of micro-decisions, which is why the philosophy seems to work when exceptional people preach it.

#### **The Seven Symptoms**

Five years ago, I documented seven biases and fallacies that cause smart product teams to build products with mediocre impact:

_The Execution Orientation Fallacy:_ We make decisions based on what we can easily execute today rather than what we should build. A team keeps iterating on their consumer mobile app because they have strong mobile engineers, even though the real opportunity is in desktop workflows for professionals.

_The Bias-for-Building Fallacy:_ We don’t thoroughly understand the problem because we feel compelled to be in constant motion. A fintech startup builds payment features for three months before discovering their target customers already have payment solutions they like—the real pain point was reporting.

_The IKEA Effect for Products:_ We base current decisions on past execution because we’re attached to work already done. A product team maintains a complex admin panel that three customers use because they spent six months building it, instead of deprecating it and focusing on the core product.

_The Focusing Illusion:_ We overestimate problem severity when talking to customers about it. Every customer interview about slow page load times makes it feel critical, but when you ship the optimization, usage barely budges because the real friction was elsewhere.

_Maslow’s Hammer:_ We overuse familiar frameworks even when inappropriate. A growth team runs A/B tests on everything including brand positioning and customer support tone, because metrics-driven optimization is their hammer.

_The Authority Approval Bias:_ We conceive features based on what will get approval rather than what customers need. A PM pitches an AI feature because the CEO mentioned AI in the last all-hands, not because customer research suggests it would help.

_Russian Roulette for Products:_ We don’t account for catastrophic scenarios. A social app ships viral sharing mechanics without considering harassment vectors, leading to a PR crisis six months later.

At the time, I suggested the solution was awareness and culture change: name these biases, create shared vocabulary, have leaders call them out upward and downward.

That solution now feels insufficient. These aren’t separate problems to be solved through better process. They’re symptoms of the deeper dynamic: average builders get attached to what they build and shackled by it. The attachment creates the bias-for-building, the IKEA effect, the authority approval bias. The shackling creates the execution orientation fallacy, the overuse of familiar hammers. You can’t eliminate these biases through awareness any more than you can eliminate gravity through awareness. They emerge from the fundamental nature of being an average builder doing the work of building.

#### **Why Failures Are Misdiagnosed**

When products fail, teams conduct postmortems. The conclusions are remarkably consistent: insufficient resources, poor market timing, inadequate engineering velocity, lack of executive support, unexpected competition, shifting strategy, missing data.

Notice what’s never blamed: thinking and judgment.

Yet every failure is ultimately a failure of thinking. Insufficient resources? Someone made poor prioritization and scoping decisions. Bad market timing? Someone misjudged market readiness. Slow engineering? Someone chose the wrong technical approach or feature set. No exec support? Someone failed to think strategically about stakeholder management.

Failures are attributed to circumstances and execution rather than thinking for predictable reasons:

It’s personally threatening to admit “we thought poorly”—it implicates everyone’s competence, especially leadership. Much safer to blame circumstances beyond our control.

Thinking is invisible. You can point to “only two engineers on the team” but you can’t point to “our collective judgment about customer needs was mediocre.”

We lack vocabulary for thinking failures. Most teams have no language for diagnosing where and how their thinking went wrong.

Organizational incentives reward leaders who “did everything right” despite failure—showed good process, worked hard, followed best practices. Admitting judgment failure offers no such protection.

The result: teams perpetually misdiagnose failures, extract wrong lessons, and repeat the same thinking errors. They hire more engineers, adjust timelines, improve their roadmapping process—and continue making the same poor micro-decisions that doom products.

#### **What This Means**

The uncomfortable truth is that most product builders are average, not exceptional. This isn’t a moral judgment—it’s statistical reality.

But recognizing this doesn’t lead to a new set of prescriptions. When you realize “just build fast” doesn’t work for most people, the natural response is to conclude its opposite—”build slowly and deliberately”—must be the answer. This is a very human tendency: when X doesn’t work, we assume the opposite of X does work.

It doesn’t. “Build less” or “build thoughtfully” are just new mantras that will fail in practice the same way “just build” fails. They’re still outcome-level philosophies that provide no guidance for the thousands of micro-decisions that constitute the actual work.

What actually matters is that you cannot shortcut judgment through mantras or frameworks. What works must be discovered in your specific situation, through practice, not adopted from theory.

This means accepting several uncomfortable realities:

You will get attached to what you build and shackled by it. This isn’t something to “combat” through awareness—it’s a force to reckon with in each specific decision you face. Knowing about the IKEA effect doesn’t prevent you from experiencing it. The question is what you do with that knowledge in the moment of decision.

The thousands of micro-decisions require contextual judgment that no framework can provide. Should you add this field to the form? It depends on your specific product, users, technical constraints, team capabilities, and strategic direction. No mantra answers this. You must develop judgment through practice and attention to what actually happens when you make decisions.

Most failures are failures of thinking, even when that’s uncomfortable to admit. The team that can honestly diagnose “our attachment to the original vision clouded our judgment about pivot timing” has a chance to develop better judgment. The team that blames “insufficient runway” will repeat the same thinking errors.

The advice from exceptional builders will mislead you. Not because they’re dishonest, but because their success comes from judgment you don’t have. You cannot become exceptional by copying their approach any more than you can become LeBron James by copying his training regimen.

The real work is developing your own judgment through practice, paying attention to what actually happens when you make decisions, and resisting the seductive comfort of mantras—whether they say “move fast” or “build thoughtfully” or anything else that promises to shortcut the hard work of figuring out what actually works in your specific circumstances.

This applies not just to product work but to every domain of human endeavor. The pattern is universal because the underlying reality is universal: exceptional talent exists, average talent is far more common, and neither adopting nor inverting the advice from the exceptional will transform average into exceptional.

The question is whether we’re willing to see it clearly and do the harder, less satisfying work of developing judgment through practice rather than seeking the next mantra that promises to make product work formulaic.
