# The Problem with Peer-reviewed Studies on Human Behavior (and a Wiser Solution)

*Peer-reviewed research on human psychology & behavior explains averages and correlations and it doesn’t say much about its applicability to you specifically. So, think for yourself.*

One mistake many smart people make is _relying_ on studies to confirm tendencies about human behavior and human psychology.

These same smart people could actually design a study to prove anything they want to prove about human behavior.

Seriously—tell me what behavior or correlation you want validated, and I can design a study that produces that result and get it published in a journal. (Within reason, of course.)

This is one of several reasons why many well-known, widely-published, very-viral studies on human behavior haven’t been successfully replicated.

[](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!remp!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F7a153f5c-ec46-48c9-a5aa-bba42de0495e_2716x1144.png)

What’s more, when it comes to studies about human behavior, all that should matter to you as an individual is _what works for you_.

Just because a study says **“78% of people behaved in <this manner>”** doesn’t mean **< this manner>** will apply to you.

And the only thing that truly matters is what works for _you_ , not what shows a positive correlation for 78% of people in a “peer-reviewed paper”.

Many otherwise-intelligent people (>130 IQ) don’t see this basic logic.

And what’s tragic is that they make poor decisions for their own life, for their own family, for their own career because they get dazzled by the statistics and the optics of such studies.

Saying things like _“Can you cite a peer-reviewed article that supports <this claim>”_ makes some people feel very intelligent, but it is simultaneously a sign that, on matters of human behavior, they are easily manipulated.

The existence of intelligence does not guarantee the presence of wisdom.

So what is the solution here?

Think for yourself.

By all means check out research, but don’t _rely_ on it.

If you’ve lived a long-enough life (say >35 years), you already have ample evidence of what’s true of human behavior. You think you don’t, but you do. This feeling that you don’t have this evidence is just your intelligent mind raising a clever objection to avoid the discomfort of thinking for yourself while still keeping its sense of intellectual superiority intact.

**now, give me 10 examples of sexy / counterintuitive / virally interesting studies on human behavior that were lauded and republished and got a lot of attention but have since failed to replicate, or, the opposite of its conclusion has been proved through another study by someone else.**

**Claude**

Here are 10 high-profile examples:

1\. Ego depletion (Baumeister) - The idea that willpower is a limited resource that gets depleted. Massive replication failures. Some recent work suggests the opposite - that belief in limited willpower causes depletion, not actual mental effort.

2\. Power posing (Cuddy) - “Stand like Wonder Woman for 2 minutes to boost testosterone and confidence.” Original findings failed to replicate. Co-author publicly distanced himself from the claims.

3\. Facial feedback hypothesis (Strack’s pen-in-teeth study) - Forcing a smile makes you happier. Major replication failure in 2016 with 17 labs.

4\. Stereotype threat - The magnitude and generalizability have been seriously questioned. Many replication attempts show much smaller effects or no effects, particularly outside specific laboratory conditions.

5\. Growth mindset interventions - Dweck’s work spawned an industry, but large-scale replication attempts show minimal to no effects in real educational settings, despite the original compelling studies.

6\. Marshmallow test (Mischel) - Reanalysis controlling for family background shows the predictive power essentially disappears. It was measuring family wealth, not self-control.

7\. Priming effects (Bargh’s elderly words → walking slowly) - Spectacular replication failures. Much of the social priming literature has collapsed.

8\. Verbal overshadowing (Schooler) - Describing a face makes you worse at recognizing it. Mixed replication results, much weaker than originally claimed.

9\. Money priming - Exposure to money concepts makes people less helpful/pro-social. Failed to replicate in many attempts, though original studies were widely cited.

10\. Hunger and judicial decisions - Judges give harsher sentences before lunch. Reanalysis suggests this was likely a statistical artifact/confound, not actual hunger effects.

Each of these was treated as revealing deep truths about human nature. Major TED talks, bestselling books, corporate training programs built on them.
