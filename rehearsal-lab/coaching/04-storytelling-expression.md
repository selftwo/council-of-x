# Expressing ideas & storytelling

Sources: `podcasts/matthew-dicks.md`, `podcasts/nancy-duarte.md`, `podcasts/andy-raskin-.md`, `podcasts/brandon-chu.md`, `podcasts/julie-zhuo.md`.

## Matthew Dicks (Storyworthy)

1. **The five-second moment.** Every story is one moment of transformation or realization ("I used to think X, stuff happened, now I think Y"); 98% of the story is context serving that moment. → *Story converges on one identifiable change, not an event sequence.*
2. **Start at the end; open in opposition.** Work backwards; the opening state is the inverse of the closing state. No "reporting on your life chronologically toward nothing." → *Opening state inverts the close.*
3. **Start close to the end.** "The shortest version of every story is the best version." If the realization happened at dessert, start with the dessert hitting the table; never mention Aruba unless it matters. → *Opens near the pivot; cuts non-serving context.*
4. **Stakes, five tools**: elephant (plant what to worry about), backpack (state the plan first), breadcrumbs (partial hints), hourglass (slow down before the payoff), crystal ball (predict a plausible bad future). "If your audience isn't wondering what you're about to say, they're no longer listening." → *Something to worry about in the first sentences; new tension mid-story.*
5. **Four attention modes: stakes, surprise, suspense, humor.** Surprise = inevitable-yet-unforeseen; hide the setup early. → *Every stretch carries at least one; setups planted early.*
6. **Business humor: nostalgia and the Sesame Street pattern** (expected, expected, unexpected). Fail-safe: if the joke misses you're still telling a story. → *Past-vs-present contrast or the unexpected third.*
7. **The dinner test.** A story should be a "slightly elevated" version of dinner-table telling. No performance-art openings. → *Sounds like elevated conversation.*
8. **It must be your story; vulnerability required.** Fold others' history in through its effect on you. "People don't want to hear spokespeople present information." → *Centers self, reveals something personal.*
9. **Personal interest inventory.** Slip relatable facts into answers ("Pretty good — my fifth graders were actually decent human beings today") rather than being "round, white and flavorless." → *Weaves one identity detail into answers.*
10. **Speak with adjacency.** Match theme, not content, then snap it over ("That's what my company does"). "We want to think about the feelings we want people to have about what we're talking about." → *Explains the abstract via a same-theme story, then snaps it to the point.*
11. **Homework for Life.** Daily one-row log of the day's most story-worthy moment; his rate went 1.8 → 7.6/day over 12 years; builds a deployable vault ("bricks, not band-aids"). → *Can produce a specific recent small moment on request.*
12. **Start with location and action.** "I am in a place and I'm doing a thing." "Location activates imagination"; it also quiets the room and wins floor space. → *First story sentence places the speaker somewhere doing something.*
13. **Storytelling is decision-making.** Prepare by listening to yourself; drill only the transitions, never memorize words; 98% of nervousness dies once you start. → *Moves between sections without stalling; recovers by naming the next scene.*

## Nancy Duarte

14. **The audience is the hero; you're the mentor.** Come alongside; empathize with how hard your ask is before making it. → *Frames the listener's difficulty before any ask; the ask helps them win.*
15. **What is / what could be / new bliss.** Oscillate current reality against the possible future; end on the world with your idea adopted. "It works in any format" — meetings, calls, chores. → *Alternates current/future state; closes with a concrete picture of after.*
16. **Can they see what you're saying?** One star moment; draw the napkin picture ("I want to draw what I see... was that your intent?"); one point per slide; sometimes kill the slides so they watch you. → *Paints or sketches a concrete image; checks alignment.*
17. **Conclusion-first vs. unpacked, by audience** (Minto adaptation). Execs get the conclusion first; other audiences "need to be taught to long for this future state." → *Adjusts punchline placement to the listener.*
18. **Tension and release aligns brains** (fMRI: teller's and listener's fire in the same order). Learn to present on something you're passionate about, then port the felt sense. → *Builds and releases tension, not a flat stream.*

## Andy Raskin

19. **Open with a shift in the world, not your product** (vs. the "arrogant doctor" pitch). Benioff: "software is over... there's this new world called the cloud." Defines a movement; the story becomes the north star for sales, product, hiring. → *Pitch opens with a change in the world.*
20. **Five parts**: name the shift (compact old-game/new-game pair), name the stakes (winners and losers), name the object of the new game as a rallying cry, name the obstacles as monsters, show the magic gifts (product as "a prop for making the story come true"). → *Names things in 2-3 words; states stakes with winners and losers.*
21. **Test whether the shift sticks.** "Am I crazy or are you seeing this?" — if they answer with their own version, it's landing. → *Invites the listener to confirm the shift in their own words.*
22. **The shit first draft.** "Having a shit draft is a million times more valuable than having all these great ideas." Slide titles are takeaways, not labels. → *Produces a rough complete version early and invites criticism.*

## Brandon Chu

23. **Writing forms the thinking.** "I figured out everything in those posts at the exact moment I wrote them." Brain-dump in two hours, then ~38 hours of editing; share raw thoughts to learn the gap between your head and how it lands. → *Restates a half-formed idea, notices where it fails, revises aloud.*
24. **You owe your team that clarity.** Even unpublished writing creates articulable clarity; external writing "was a better way to influence internally... accelerated my career probably a decade." → *Positions arrive pre-structured, not thought out in circles.*
25. **Narrate long feedback cycles.** Platform work needs the big narrative — connect the unglamorous alpha API to how it changes merchants' lives. → *Frames incremental work inside a larger arc.*

## Julie Zhuo

26. **Publish 52 times a year to cure large-room silence.** Only goal: hit publish. "I'm the number one audience for my own writing." By year-end, "a huge difference in my ability to speak up in large meetings." → *Voices a position in group settings without waiting for perfect.*
27. **The enumeration skill.** She admired colleagues who compress chaos live: "The problem is one, blah, two, blah, three." A year of thread-writing taught her to think "1, 2, 3" naturally. → *Summarizes a messy discussion as 2-3 numbered points, in the moment.*
28. **Separate generation from editing** (NaNoWriMo). Volume first, shape later; the blank page is the hardest part. → *Gives a rough answer immediately and refines, instead of freezing.*
29. **Lead feedback with the problem, not the solution.** "When everyone is aligned on the problem, we collectively come up with better solutions." Announce which layer you want feedback on. → *Names problem and evidence before proposing the fix.*

## Convergences worth teaching as one principle

- **Contrast structure**: Dicks's opening-vs-end, Duarte's what-is/could-be, Raskin's old-game/new-game.
- **Assume no one wants to listen; earn attention**: Dicks's stakes, Duarte's audience-as-hero.
- **Writing as thinking**: Chu, Zhuo, and Lenny's Hemingway epigraph.
