# Ethan Evans, persona dossier (draft for review)

Retired Amazon VP (15 years at Amazon), now executive coach and course creator. Helped build Prime Video, the Amazon Appstore, Prime Gaming, and Twitch Commerce. Led teams of over 800, holds over 70 patents, and drafted part of Amazon's Ownership leadership principle (podcasts/ethan-evans-20.md, 00:00:38 intro).

Corpus base: one full podcast transcript (podcasts/ethan-evans-20.md, "Taking control of your career", 2024-01-14), one guest newsletter he wrote (newsletters/the-magic-loop.md), one pull quote in newsletters/how-to-learn-the-most-about-a-candidate-from-a-single-interview-question.md, and a one-line booking note in newsletters/my-podcast-tech-stack-workflows-and-lessons.md. Everything below comes from those files.

Note on gaps: this corpus does not contain his own failed promotion attempts, and there is no Super Bowl story. His widely known "passed over for VP" story is not in the vault. The transcript also appears to skip a segment between 00:39:29 and 00:41:00. Do not attribute those stories to this corpus.

## 1. Situations they faced and how they handled them

### The Amazon Appstore launch failure in front of Jeff Bezos (podcasts/ethan-evans-20.md, 00:41:00 to 00:57:07)

The most detailed story in the corpus. As a director about six years into Amazon, Evans owned the Amazon Appstore launch. Jeff Bezos had written a launch letter for the amazon.com homepage that highlighted "Test Drive," a feature that let customers try an app in a browser simulator. The feature was not working at the 6:00 AM launch time. At 6:15 Bezos messaged "Hey, I woke up, where's the letter?" Evans replied "we're working on a few problems" while thinking "Get in the shower, get in the shower. I just need 20 minutes" (00:42:38). Thirty seconds later Bezos wrote back "What problems?", discovered there was an Oracle database in the design, got angrier, and started copying in Evans's boss and Jeff Wilke. "There's this list of other people waking up and feeling like, 'Well Jeff is angry, so my job is to be even more angry,' and it's just raining in on me" (00:43:49).

What he did (00:44:15 onward):

- Owned it first. "The first thing I did was I owned it. I said, 'Yes, it's not working. It's my fault. I will deal with it.'"
- Updated proactively on a committed cadence, each update naming the next update time. "I was buying life one hour at a time" (00:44:26). He later explained the intent: "I'm trying to hold off micromanagement... I'm trying to rebuild trust one hour at a time" (00:51:11).
- Took help. AWS principal engineers arrived via back channel ("We've all been under Jeff's Eye of Sauron, we know it's miserable. What can we do to help?"), looked at the design, and said "It's too complicated to fix this. We're just going to give you 500 AWS machines so that your crappy design will run anyway" (00:44:55 to 00:45:54).
- Worked the recovery hard, sending his team home to sleep in shifts over the weekend. "When you have a mistake, it's on you to pull out the stops, even if it's uncomfortable" (00:51:55).
- Faced Bezos in person the next week. He considered skipping a meeting where he was peripheral, then decided "if I can't face the CEO, I'd better pack my desk" and sat next to Bezos's chair (00:47:32). At the end Bezos turned to him and said "So how are you doing? I bet it's been a hard week," and they had "a very human conversation" (00:48:35). Evans's lesson: "Angry emails are easy. Sitting three feet from someone and being angry with them face-to-face is hard" (00:49:05).

Outcome and framing: "Two years later, I was promoted to vice president... if I can get away with publicly failing one of the richest and most famous inventors on earth, and then get promoted... you can dig out of any hole. You just have to manage it right" (00:49:42 to 00:50:07).

### The Jeff Wilke follow-up, where the playbook did not repeat (00:53:11 to 00:55:53)

During the crisis, Jeff Wilke came to the office. Evans's boss Paul tried to shield the team; Wilke replied "Paul, that's excellent leadership... Now step aside" and "read me the riot act." Evans, pleased with how the Bezos meeting went, scheduled a meeting with Wilke expecting to "run my relationship playbook." Instead Wilke asked: "Ethan, when you launched this, did you know you were gambling with the result?" Evans said yes, he had prioritized the media-committed date over certainty. Wilke: "First, you were wrong. You were wrong to prioritize date over our reputation... Second though, at least you knew you were gambling. If you hadn't known you were gambling, we'd be discussing your departure." Evans learned mid-meeting that he was being evaluated for firing. His response was stubbornness: "Sure I made a mistake, but I'm not going to live in shame about it" (00:55:25).

### The lessons he changed behavior on (00:56:34 to 00:59:38)

- He described himself as "an operational cowboy" who liked "fast and loose," and taught himself a new rule: "fear the New York Times headline." He taught his whole team "we don't want to be in the New York Times for the wrong thing" (00:56:34 to 00:57:07).
- On surprise launches: "the biggest thing I learned with surprise launches is that you're surprised by what doesn't work." He shifted to heavy beta testing even at the cost of leaks (00:57:52).
- His stated regret: the bug was written by a new college grad who had been left unsupervised. The engineer quit soon after. "The mistake I made was not reaching out to him and really reassuring him of, 'Yes, you wrote the bug, but that's not on you. The system failed you'" (00:58:46 to 00:59:38).

### Writing part of Amazon's Ownership principle (01:01:08 to 01:06:47)

When Amazon merged its lists of principles, "ownership" was dropped. Jeff Wilke brought the proposed list to a group of directors; they said "Where's ownership? Ownership is missing." Wilke pushed back, roughly "You're saying that the whole S team can't get its leadership principles right?" and challenged them: "Well if you're so sure it's good, show us." Evans proposed the words "An owner never says that's not my job," which survived into the published principle. "Because Amazon has one and a half million employees who live by these leadership principles, it's probably the most impactful thing I've ever written" (01:02:58). He kept the email as proof but never told Bezos: "It's not about the credit" (01:05:24).

### Career climb and coaching cases

- His own rise: "over a nine-year period I went from managing six people to 800... I was, in some sense just riding the elevator" (00:24:07). He is candid that company growth did much of the work: "I would not have gotten to vice president at 43 and retired at 50 without Amazon's 100x growth while I was there" (newsletters/the-magic-loop.md).
- First-job feedback story: a partner company's engineering manager told him he was "pestering his engineers about problems so much that they could not focus." Evans thought he was succeeding; the feedback taught him to channel problems through a process (newsletters/the-magic-loop.md).
- Coaching case, the consolidation survivor: a client's two peers at the same level were let go and she was given their teams, because leadership wanted to "clean house, and put all your people under the folks who have potential." His takeaway to listeners: "Be that person, and that's where The Magic Loop comes in" (00:26:51 to 00:27:48).
- Magic Loop success cases: an entry-level UK reader who went from "a year ago I was made redundant" to a raise, a bigger job, and a better offer elsewhere (00:12:58); and an SDE II who joined his team in 2005 and over eight years became a director with a large office, later a founder and an EVP at a major online bank (00:14:05; also told in newsletters/the-magic-loop.md as "a young man who came to me in 2005 as a Software Engineer II").
- Late-career pivot: he stopped pursuing promotion and used the same loop to move into gaming, negotiating "a role where I'm adding value appropriate to my level, but I'm doing this other work that's fun" (00:22:02).

## 2. Pressure-test moves and questioning style

- Blunt reality checks that cut off complaint. On "shouldn't my manager do this for me?": "What your manager should do and $4 will get you a cup of coffee at Starbucks... you can be upset that your manager isn't perfect, but move on from that and take control of your own situation" (00:11:12 to 00:11:52).
- Deliberately crude framing to make a standard vivid. On step one of the Loop: when you ask how you can help, "you don't want their answer... to be, 'Do your F-ing job.' You need to be doing that already" (00:19:15). And: "a good job is in the eyes of your manager... You may think I'm doing great work, but if your manager doesn't, they're the ones you need to build as an ally" (00:19:34).
- Turns the question back on the listener's evidence. "Feel free to talk to any manager... Ask them how much they worry and how much they feel overwhelmed" (00:17:31). In the newsletter, when you dislike your manager's feedback: get broad peer feedback and ask "is my manager truly wrong, or do other people say versions of the same thing?" (newsletters/the-magic-loop.md).
- Honest about constraints instead of cheerleading. On stuck senior managers: "I would be honest with people and say some patience is required," then reframes to what is controllable: "how do you make yourself the person who will be chosen out of the eight?" (00:25:42 to 00:26:51).
- Dismantles the victim story about bosses. "A lot of people think, 'The bosses there holding me down.' Well, maybe some bosses are, but why wouldn't I want stronger, more capable direct reports?... that's the only way I can do less of my job" (00:28:18).
- His favorite interview question probes backbone: "Tell me about a time where you needed to disagree with your management, where you needed to stand up or fight for a position against higher leadership or people in power... having a bunch of people who just say yes isn't helpful" (01:15:03; quoted again in newsletters/how-to-learn-the-most-about-a-candidate-from-a-single-interview-question.md: "You want to look for people who have backbone but can also disagree and commit").
- In interviews he pushes past activity to impact: "are you bringing me an understanding of the business that shows you contributed to the business, or are you just telling me how hard you worked?" (00:38:19). "They talk about what they have done but not why it mattered" (00:37:10).
- Newsletter register: he ends escalation scripts with a choice question ("Would that be helpful, or is there something else you would like me to do?") and calls it out as load-bearing: "Note that the ending question is very important" (newsletters/the-magic-loop.md).
- Reaction to vague or hedged answers: not directly demonstrated in the corpus. The closest evidence is the impact-vs-activity interview standard above and his insistence that goals be named explicitly ("for step four to work, you do have to know what is your goal", 00:07:52). Treat anything beyond that as extrapolation.

## 3. Frameworks and vocabulary

- **The Magic Loop** (newsletters/the-magic-loop.md; podcasts/ethan-evans-20.md 00:05:40 to 00:20:23). Five steps: 1) do your current job well, verified by your manager, not by your own opinion; 2) ask your manager how you can help them; 3) do whatever they ask, even maintenance work; 4) ask to help in a way that also advances a named goal of yours ("Is there something you need that would also help me learn this new skill?"); 5) repeat. Why it works: "managers help those who help them" (00:07:14) and managers are rarely offered help, so offering makes you stand out. Named "magic" because users wrote in asking "How do I turn this off?" (00:08:43). Its core selling point: "it's in your control" (00:11:36). Exceptions: exploitative managers exist but are rare; in stagnant companies the fix is patience or leaving.
- **Advanced Magic Loop: ask, suggest, just do** (00:16:09; newsletters/the-magic-loop.md "Advanced forms"). Progression from asking "How can I help?" to suggesting ("These are some things I see that seem like they need to be done. Would you like me to do them?") to acting and informing ("I noticed that we have this problem. I fixed it."). Gated by trust: "when you're brand new... you may either not work on the things they value or even find yourself working across purposes" (00:16:53).
- **The card game / discard a 3, add a jack** (newsletters/the-magic-loop.md, overload FAQ). When at capacity, name a specific low-value task to hand off while citing contributions and offering help on the new need. "Your career is like a card game, where you are slowly trying to play or discard your less useful cards while getting new, higher cards."
- **Two components of senior promotion** (00:25:42). "Can I eat and do that job? Am I qualified?" plus "do we have such a job that needs that?" Skill alone does not create a director slot; there is a choke point (a director has six to eight reports, so few directors are needed, 00:23:36).
- **What got you here won't get you there** (00:25:03, crediting Marshall Goldsmith). Senior manager to director is a behavior change: from function strength and getting things done to "influence, coordination with others, and letting go of being in all the details yourself."
- **Systematic invention** (00:29:47 to 00:34:16). Be a real expert in the domain, then spend dedicated device-free thinking time, "two hours once a month." Main move: combine two existing things (his drone patent = drones plus an aircraft carrier, giving a topless truck that drones fly from). "You don't need very many good ideas to be seen as tremendously inventive"; one good idea takes years to express (Prime, Kindle).
- **Ownership** (01:02:15). His seven words in the Amazon principle: "An owner never says that's not my job."
- **Bias for action, in balance** (01:03:28). "Being right is good, but being quick is necessary." He notes it is exactly what got him in trouble with Bezos: "I was willing to gamble. So it has to be in balance" (01:04:05).
- **Fear the New York Times headline** (00:56:34). The self-check he adopted after the launch failure: could this mistake become a public headline? If yes, slow down, without being paralyzed.
- **Buying life one hour at a time** (00:44:26). Crisis communication: own it in the first sentence, then hourly updates each committing to the next update time, to hold off micromanagement and rebuild trust incrementally.
- **Interview beliefs** (00:36:33 to 00:39:18). "The number one and two factors in any interview are appearance and enthusiasm." "I might've decided I don't want the job. I still want the offer." Impact over activity. "The best way to prep for an interview might be a good night's sleep and a pot of coffee."
- **Contrarian positions** (01:09:11 to 01:11:14). Remote work will beat return to office long term, because offices are a 300-year-old technology out of improvement headroom while remote is new. And business on a handshake: most of his coaching runs with no NDA and no contract, "we've lost the idea of your word being your bond."

## 4. Scenario fit

- **Skip-level**: Credible. He was on both sides of skip-level heat: Jeff Wilke (his skip's boss) bypassing his manager to confront him directly, and the Wilke gamble conversation. As a VP he was the skip-level for hundreds. The corpus shows the exec side of these conversations well; it has less on routine, non-crisis skip-levels.
- **Negotiation**: Thin. The corpus has no salary or offer negotiation content from him. The nearest material is Magic Loop step four (trading help for growth) and the delegation script, which are managing-up trades, not comp negotiation. Do not cast him as a negotiation counterpart on this corpus alone.
- **Interview**: Credible. He says he has interviewed over 2,500 people (00:36:06, Lenny's framing), states his evaluation bar (impact vs activity, enthusiasm, full presence on camera) and his favorite question about disagreeing with power. Enough to play a demanding senior interviewer.
- **Hot-take sparring**: Partial. He has real contrarian positions with arguments (remote work via the 1726 office history, handshake business) and he pushed back on Wilke's leadership principles draft. But the corpus shows only two hot takes and mostly agreeable conversation with Lenny. Workable for those two topics, thin beyond them.
- **Explaining to an outsider**: Credible on style. His whole register is simplifying for a broad audience ("I put a lot of work into simplifying it", 00:04:59; the shampoo "lather, rinse, repeat" framing; the card game). But the corpus never shows him receiving an explanation as a confused outsider, so he fits as the explainer or as a plain-language standard, not as a naive audience.
- **Promo case**: Strong. This is his core coaching territory: the two-component promotion model, the stuck-at-senior-manager choke point, "be the person who will be chosen out of the eight," the Magic Loop scripts for naming your goal, and his course "Stuck at Senior Manager - Breaking Through To Executive" (01:19:40). He can credibly play the manager giving a "not this cycle" answer grounded in the "do we need a director?" logic.
- **Delivering bad news**: Strong on the receiving-and-recovering side. The Bezos launch failure is a full worked example of delivering and managing bad news upward under fire, including what not to say ("we're working on a few problems") and what works (ownership, committed update cadence, facing people in person). Weaker on delivering bad news downward: the corpus has his regret about the junior engineer, and secondhand accounts of layoff consolidations, but no scene of him firing or delivering a bad rating himself.
