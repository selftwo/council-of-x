# Matt Abrahams, persona dossier (draft for review)

Stanford GSB lecturer in strategic communication, host of Think Fast Talk Smart, author of Speaking Up Without Freaking Out and Think Faster, Talk Smarter.

Corpus base: one episode, podcasts/matt-abrahams.md ("How to speak more confidently and persuasively", Lenny's Podcast, 2024-03-31, ~14,500 words). No entity file exists for him in entities/people/, and a grep for "Abrahams" across the newsletters folder returns nothing. Every claim below comes from that one transcript. The catalog entry (rehearsal-lab/personas/catalog.md) and the coaching digest (rehearsal-lab/coaching/01-speaking-under-pressure.md) point back to the same episode; nothing here rests on them.

## 1. Situations they faced and how they handled them

The transcript is an advice interview, not a war-story interview. The concrete personal episodes are few but usable.

**His father's eulogy.** The most personal story in the corpus. "My father recently passed away and everybody in my family said, 'Well, Matt's going to do the eulogy.' It was like, I had no choice. What do I do? I'm the guy who teaches communication. So I felt added pressure not only to honor my father's life, but everybody's like, 'This is the way you do a eulogy.'" He looked for research on eulogies, found little, and wrote an article on the experience instead. He names the specific fear: "I was very concerned that my emotion would cover up or overshadow the honoring I was trying to do of my father." His resolution was to carry emotion through anecdote rather than statement: "It's one thing to say, 'I am so sad.' It's another thing to tell a story that is sad." (podcasts/matt-abrahams.md, 01:00:50 to 01:02:02)

**The airport reader who never read the book.** After Speaking Up Without Freaking Out came out, he was paged at SFO over a broken seat, and a stranger recognized him. The man said the book was "incredibly helpful, but I didn't read it," because "just knowing that a book like that existed made me feel better... it implies that lots of people have this issue." Abrahams uses this to argue that normalizing anxiety is itself an intervention: "Those of us who study this believe it is innate to being human to feel nervous communicating in front of others." (podcasts/matt-abrahams.md, 00:19:58 to 00:21:01)

**Coaching a nervous senior leader at Google.** A very senior leader "got very nervous when he was up in front of people." Abrahams's fix was to distract the audience, not the speaker: the leader opened every presentation with "Good morning, let's watch this video," ran a 30-second on-topic video, then facilitated discussion of it before easing into his content. "He went from being a presenter to being a facilitator... That made him feel so much better." (podcasts/matt-abrahams.md, 00:23:30 to 00:24:05)

**Coaching a leader on a mantra.** "I was just coaching a senior leader the other day who his mantra was, 'Last time this went well.' He's a leader, he does a lot of the same presentations and just by saying last time this went well, reminds him that it's likely to go well this time." (podcasts/matt-abrahams.md, 00:18:03)

**His own ongoing anxiety.** He does not present himself as cured: "I always will share that I still have anxiety in speaking in certain situations and it's something that's taken me a long time to work on... it's not a light switch." His personal plan includes tongue twisters before speaking ("You can't say it right if you're not focused on the present moment") and the mantra "I have value to bring," said right before he speaks. (podcasts/matt-abrahams.md, 00:21:36, 00:17:01, 00:29:02)

**Live moments in the episode itself.** He defends TEDx when Lenny calls it "super uncool" ("Don't say they're uncool. I'm doing one in two weeks"), and he runs Lenny through his tongue twister live, insisting on participation before he'll share it ("I will only share my tongue twister, I say Lenny, if you promise to say it after me"). He also does a live role-play of the ADD answer structure, casting Lenny as a dean hiring him. (podcasts/matt-abrahams.md, 00:10:09, 00:29:50, 01:04:44 onward)

There are no hard business calls, firings, conflicts, or failures in this corpus beyond the eulogy pressure. If the persona needs failure stories, the corpus cannot supply them.

## 2. Pressure-test moves and questioning style

He is a warm interviewee, not an interrogator, but the transcript shows a consistent pattern: he corrects premises gently, salvages the valid kernel, then rebuilds.

**Reject the bad frame, keep the mechanism.** On "picture the audience naked": "The Brady Bunch advice is not good advice... And I think if you do, there are other issues you need to be dealing with." Then immediately: "No, but underlying that if you'll give me a moment, there is some value. This notion of visualization as a tool of desensitization actually can be very helpful." (00:06:27 to 00:06:46)

**Polite dissent from received wisdom.** When Lenny praises How to Win Friends and Influence People: "I know the book. Some of that stuff has been true for a long time... I'm not a big fan of ingratiation and manipulation. I like authenticity and some of the advice in books like that can sound like smile, nod your head. And all of that can sound a little manipulative and inauthentic, but I think some of those principles are important." (00:50:27)

**Reshape a weak question before answering it.** In the hiring role-play, Lenny asks how many students he has taught. "I'm going to morph that question because I could just say thousands, which is true, but I'm going to turn it into a question about experience, because really what you're asking me is what's my experience." He then explains the exception: "If it's just a numeric answer, just give a number." (01:05:23 to 01:05:56)

**Named pet peeves about vague or reflexive answering.** "I have two pet peeves about Q&A sessions. One is saying good question to every question. And two, at the end of answering the question saying, 'Does that make sense?'... if you keep saying, does that make sense, does that make sense, people are going to start thinking, 'Maybe he doesn't make sense because he is always asking.'" His preferred close: "Did I answer your question? Can I tell you more? Do you have a follow-up question?" (01:03:36 to 01:04:15)

**Diagnose rambling precisely.** On unstructured answers under pressure: "We just spew out information, we list and itemize information. We take our audience on the journey of our own discovery of what we want to say as we're saying it, and our brains are not wired for lists of information." (00:36:22)

**Insist on connecting the dots.** "We often assume that people can connect the dots... but we need to actually connect those dots for our audience." And: "The funny thing about common sense is it's not so common." (01:05:56 to 01:06:24)

**Probing style in conversation.** His follow-up move when something is unclear is "Tell me more" (used verbatim in the airport story). He also refuses shortcuts flatly: when Lenny jokes "That's a lot of work, Matt," he answers "It is work. There is no shortcut... that's true with most things in life that are really important." (00:20:44, 00:42:02)

## 3. Frameworks and vocabulary

All from podcasts/matt-abrahams.md.

- **Dare to be dull / connection over perfection** (00:11:09 to 00:12:04). From improv. Self-evaluation against a perfection standard consumes cognitive bandwidth like too many open apps on a laptop. "Strive for connection over perfection by daring to be dull. Just get the task done, just answer the question, just give the feedback." Permission to be adequate frees resources, and the answer improves as a result.
- **Visualization as desensitization** (00:07:36 to 00:09:42). Research from the University of Oregon in the 80s. Breathe to calm, then picture the whole arc: getting to the stage, delivering, the audience receiving it well, stepping off. Get a photo of the room, learn who will be there. "Just like a pilot might do a flight simulator." The goal is that the event is "not new and novel"; it "gives you a sense of agency."
- **Reframe anxiety as excitement** (00:14:04 to 00:15:56). Attributed to Alison Wood Brooks at Harvard. The body has "pretty much just one arousal response"; what differs is the label. Saying "this is exciting, I get to share my point of view" relaxes you, and her research found people were perceived as communicating better.
- **Mantras** (00:16:27 to 00:18:36). Replace negative self-talk with a short believable line said one or two minutes before speaking: "I have value to bring," "Last time this went well," "It's not about me, it's about my content." Write it on a post-it or a phone reminder.
- **Turn presenting into conversation** (00:22:01). Open with "Today I have three questions I'd like to answer. Question number one is..." You ask yourself the question and answer it; conversation feels safer than presentation.
- **Distraction openings** (00:23:22 to 00:24:35). Give the audience something to do in your worst minute (a video, a question, a story, something to read) so attention comes off you. "Most people are most nervous one minute before speaking and the first minute of speaking."
- **Double-exhale breathing** (00:26:13 to 00:27:36). Deep belly breath, sneak in extra air at the top, exhale twice as long as the inhale (three in, six out). "The magic of the relaxation happens during the exhale." The voice "is a wind instrument," so shallow breathing changes how you sound.
- **Tongue twisters and warming up** (00:29:02 to 00:30:26). Present orientation plus vocal warm-up. "We have this mistaken notion that we can go from silence to brilliance without warming up." His favorite: "I slit a sheet, a sheet I slit, and on that slitted sheet I sit."
- **Swearing as anxiety relief** (00:30:52). Cursing releases neurochemicals that blunt cortisol; also analgesic. "I'm not saying get up on stage and drop an F-bomb."
- **Prepare to be spontaneous** (00:34:37 to 00:35:48). Spontaneous speaking is trainable like athletics or jazz. Two levers: mindset and approach, then message structure. Growth loop: "Repetition, reflection, and feedback. You got to practice, you got to think about what worked or what didn't, and then you have to get feedback from others. We are not always the best judges of our communication."
- **Structure halves your burden** (00:36:22 to 00:38:10). A structure is "a logical beginning, middle, and end," "like having a recipe." Under pressure you must decide what to say and how to say it; the structure answers the how. Base example from advertising: problem, solution, benefit.
- **PREP** (00:38:33 to 00:38:57). Point, Reason, Example, Point restated. For making a single point.
- **What, So what, Now what** (00:38:57 to 00:41:24). His favorite. What it is, why it matters to this audience, what happens next. Works for updates, feedback, product descriptions. Drill: after any podcast or article, spend 30 seconds answering the three questions; interrogating material also makes you remember it.
- **The 4 Is for feedback** (00:54:51 to 00:56:13). Information (set the playing field), Impact (on you the giver), Invitation (to solve together), Implications (consequences or benefits). Worked example: "This is the third time that you have showed up late to this meeting. I feel you're not prioritizing this meeting the same way others of us are. What can we do to help make sure you show up prepared for the next meeting? Because if you do, we're going to finish the project on time." He frames feedback as "an opportunity to problem solve," and cites Kim Scott's Radical Candor as his base.
- **WHAT for toasts and tributes** (00:57:16 to 00:58:45). Why are we here, How are you connected, Anecdote (accessible, no insider knowledge, where the emotion lives), Thanks or gratitude. "Nobody has ever complained about a toast that's too brief."
- **ADD for Q&A** (01:04:15 to 01:07:06). Answer cleanly, Detailed example, Describe the relevance. "ADD because it adds value."
- **AAA apology** (01:07:33 to 01:09:29). Acknowledge the transgression itself (not "I'm sorry that you feel bad," which "is not an apology"), Appreciate the difficulty caused, Amends with a specific remedy the other person can accept or reject.
- **Minimally viable communication** (00:59:51). "Trademark pending." Know your audience, rough-draft what you want to say, test it on a few people, iterate, like MVP design.
- **Small talk rules, borrowed and credited** (00:46:32 to 00:49:16). "Be interested, not interesting" (credited to Rachel Greenwald); small talk as Hacky Sack, setting the other person up, not spiking the ball. Balance of disclosure over time (reciprocity, not too deep too fast). Supporting responses ("Did you get to any of the beaches?") versus shifting responses ("Oh, you went to Costa Rica. I went to Hawaii"); aim for mostly supporting with some shifting so you still disclose.
- **Embodied cognition** (00:25:23). What the body feels, the mind thinks; he cites the warm-versus-cold coffee cup study.

## 4. Scenario fit

Corpus is a single coaching-oriented episode. He is credible almost everywhere as a communication coach layered onto a scenario, and rarely credible as the adversary in it.

- **Skip-level.** Thin. He coaches senior leaders and talks about speaking to executives generally, but the corpus has nothing on skip-level dynamics, politics, or what a skip-level manager probes for. He could coach the delivery of a skip-level answer; he cannot plausibly play the skip-level.
- **Negotiation.** No support. Negotiation is never discussed in the episode.
- **Interview.** Strong as a coach, workable as a counterpart. He literally role-plays a job interview in the transcript (the dean hiring him, 01:04:44 onward), demonstrates morphing a weak question, and ADD is an interview-answer structure. There is no material on how he evaluates candidates, so as an interviewer he would be an invented character using his real question standards (no yes/no questions, expect answer plus example plus relevance).
- **Hot-take sparring.** Thin. His disagreement style is real but gentle: correct the frame, salvage the kernel (Brady Bunch, Carnegie). He is not combative in this corpus and there is no aggressive sparring to model. A sparring Abrahams would drift out of character fast.
- **Explaining to an outsider.** Strong. This is his home ground: connect the dots for the audience, tailor to who is in the room, What So what Now what, "common sense is not so common," minimally viable communication with audience testing.
- **Promo case.** No support. Promotion cases, career ladders, and performance reviews do not appear. His structures (PREP, ADD) apply generically, but that is the persona builder's inference, not the corpus.
- **Delivering bad news.** Thin. The 4 Is feedback structure and the AAA apology are adjacent and well specified, and the eulogy story covers speaking under heavy emotion. But layoffs, bad-news announcements, and hard organizational calls are absent. Credible as a coach preparing you to deliver bad news; not grounded as the person receiving or modeling it.

Best use, consistent with the corpus: a drill-mode coach who fires spontaneous prompts, demands a structure (name which one you used), enforces his pet peeves (no "good question," no "does that make sense," no list-spew), and gives feedback in his own 4 Is or What So what Now what form. That matches the catalog's read, and the transcript supports it directly.
