# Alisa Cohn dossier (draft for review)

Persona: Alisa Cohn, executive coach to startup founders and CEOs. Clients named in the episode intro include Etsy, Wirecutter, Venmo, DraftKings, Microsoft, Google, Pfizer, and the New York Times. Named a top 50 coach by Thinkers50 and number one startup coach by Global Gurus (podcasts/alisa-cohn.md, 00:01:00). Author of a book with scripts for hard conversations and host of the podcast "From Start-Up to Grown-Up" (podcasts/alisa-cohn.md, 00:16:42 and 01:22:51).

Corpus base: a single Lenny's Podcast transcript, podcasts/alisa-cohn.md ("Scripts for difficult conversations", 2025-01-05, ~17k words). There is no entity file for her in entities/people/, and a grep of newsletters/ finds no Alisa Cohn coverage (the only "Cohn" hit is Zachary Cohn, a different person). Everything below comes from that one transcript. That is a real limitation: it is rich on method and scripts, thinner on long war stories.

## 1. Situations they faced and how they handled them

**The client who "she's just going to cry" (00:11:05 to 00:12:23).** A client running a division kept avoiding feedback to an underperformer: "She's just going to cry. She's older, whatever... it's going to be too uncomfortable." Cohn worked him through it: "We talked and talked and talked. I gave him a script. We really worked it out." He had the conversation. The woman cried, went home early. The next day she came in and said, "Thank you so much for telling me that. I wish someone had told me that 15 years ago. I think I could have had a different career." Cohn's takeaway: "the only way you're going to be able to help someone grow in their career and become the best person they can be is by leaning into these tough conversations."

**The avocado toast culture (00:41:57 to 00:44:56).** A sincere founder who "wants to do right by his workforce" built rituals: 10 AM avocado toast, longer hangouts, evening socials, eventually a culture committee. But expectations stayed unclear, results were not showing, and "there began to be kind of a cliquey, gossipy culture of who's in and who's out." Her diagnosis: he had not codified culture. "Culture is not just avocado toast and working together and having socials, culture is things like, we go the extra mile... we measure twice and cut once." Her method: surface his underlying assumptions ("there's a reason everyone does what they do"), then he "had to really courageously make some changes." They parted ways with "one or two really toxic people" and "when they all got on the same page, they were able to gain a lot more traction."

**The visionary founder who would not adjust (00:46:01 to 00:47:30).** A founder wanted to be a visionary, inspirational leader, but "what her company needed was somebody to structure and hold people accountable and help them create goals and achieve milestones and course correct." She was frustrated when people got off course but did not see that fixing it "was, in one way or the other, her job." Cohn talked to her a lot about getting a COO partner. Outcome, honestly reported: "she did not adjust her style. And so there's a lot of wheel spinning that happens from that." Cohn does not pretend every engagement works.

**The weekend Slack cofounder conflict (00:59:50 to 01:00:25).** One founder Slacked his cofounder on weekends; the cofounder never responded, which was "extremely frustrating." When they finally addressed it, it "really was about wanting to have some downtime and some, quote unquote, 'Balance.'" Her point: "because they didn't talk about it, both sides made a big assumption about it and then it caused this conflict that didn't have to happen if they'd had the conversation in advance."

**The cofounders who never aligned on ambition (01:02:40 to 01:03:33).** Five or six years in, one cofounder told her wistfully, "Gosh, I don't see why we have to grow... I would just much prefer an environment where we didn't have to grow." But the company was already venture backed and the other cofounder "had a very lofty ambition for a very big company." "It was way too late to even have that conversation and it was a very painful reckoning for both of them." This is her core evidence for the founder prenup, along with the stat she cites: "65% of startups fail because of conflict with founders or the founding team" (attributed to "Noam, Noam Wasserstein", 00:56:41; she likely means Noam Wasserman, but that is her wording in the transcript).

**Her own failure 1: the fetal position on the floor (01:09:04 to 01:10:32).** Early in her coaching practice, after a conversation that went badly, the voice in her head was "What will become of me?" "I got onto the floor, my hardwood floors in my Brookline condo, and I just balled in the fetal position... it wasn't 10 minutes, it was an hour." Then a "stress nap," then "I just started making more calls and doing more things." Lesson in her words: "when you keep taking action, action, action, win or lose, win or lose, you'll get where you need to go."

**Her own failure 2: the offsite the client wanted to end (01:10:56 to 01:12:31).** Early in her career an offsite was going badly and during a break the client said, "I just think we should end this offsite... it's not working." She felt "horrible, obviously, humiliated, certainly." Her response: she took training, worked with a mentor, rebuilt every part of her offsite practice (aligning with the client in advance, right activities, goal focus), and "got so great at offsites after that experience." Her framing: "anything that you're learning from that, can then be turned into fuel to build your skills to get great at the thing that you're not great at."

**The CEO hiding behind patience (01:13:10 to 01:15:09).** A CEO watched a launch keep slipping and told himself "you need to have patience with it as it goes." Cohn had talked to people around him and saw "a massive process problem": an inexperienced product manager "was kind of hiding it because he knew he didn't have the skills, was fighting with engineering." She "had a long discussion... where I kind of enlightened him about some of the issues that he needed to get involved and fix." Her rule: "sometimes you need to have patience and sometimes you need to look at the process," and the tell is "if you search your mind, you don't really know how this thing is going to come together... you kind of hear this uncomfortable silence about it."

**The manager who only wanted negative feedback (00:36:40).** A manager in one of her trainings said, "I don't like getting positive feedback. I only like getting negative feedback... Negative feedback, you can learn something." Cohn uses this to argue that praise must be held to the same standard of specificity as criticism.

## 2. Pressure-test moves and questioning style

The best evidence is that she coaches Lenny live on air, unprompted, within the first minute of the scripts discussion (00:05:32 to 00:06:48). The sequence:

1. She flips the interview: "If I could ask you a question, if you're picturing a difficult conversation that you have had, should have, might have... can you sum it up? What's hard about it? Because it's helpful to clarify what is hard about it?"
2. When Lenny gives a compound answer (making people upset, making things worse), she forces a choice: "Okay, about making things worse or about their reaction?"
3. Then she drills one level deeper: "What's the problem if they're sad and upset? What does that mean to you?"
4. When Lenny runs out of answer, she releases the pressure rather than grinding: "You can think about it some more, right? I'm not going to put you on the spot right now." Then she generalizes the lesson: "we're putting meaning on things all the time... if you can get to the bottom of what you're putting on top of it, what you're weighting it with."

Other characteristic moves in the transcript:

- **Evidence demand.** "I always ask my clients, what's my evidence that this is happening?" (00:19:52). Feedback must rest on observable facts, not feelings.
- **Reading hedges as no.** On whether a client has been clear before firing someone: "Regularly with my clients, I'll say, 'Okay, have you been crystal clear about what you need from this person?' And what they always do is the hand motion like well, sort of, but well, maybe. Which means no, which means no." (00:31:43). She treats a hedged answer as a plain no and says so.
- **Simple "how come" probes.** To the manager who disliked positive feedback: "And I said, 'How come?'" (00:36:40). Short, open, non-leading.
- **Checking the work after handing over a script.** With the crying-employee client: gives the script, gets agreement he will do it, and he "reported back to me" afterward (00:11:05). She closes the loop.
- **Her stated coaching philosophy:** "listen to people more deeply and ask deeper questions, not just respond to what they just said, but why do you think that? Or where is that coming from?... get really beneath the surface" (01:20:38). Lenny notes on air that "your energy just changed into coaching mode when you said that."
- **Soliciting reactions to her own scripts.** After delivering the pre-firing script she asks Lenny, "What do you think of that?" (00:33:22).

Tone throughout: warm, brisk, matter of fact. She repeatedly normalizes ("you're not alone," "no one wants to make anybody upset") before pushing, and she pushes by narrowing questions rather than by contradiction.

## 3. Frameworks and vocabulary

All from podcasts/alisa-cohn.md.

**Scripts for difficult conversations (00:12:55 onward).** Her core method. Three-part prep: (1) tap into what is uncomfortable for you about it, (2) get your mindset right (you are doing this to help them, not to vent; if you are venting, "maybe not do it then, until you can transform your reasoning," 00:09:50), (3) "really be able to wrap your mouth around the words," which means practicing the actual script out loud. Scripts must be made your own: "I'll land it for them. But they have to make it their own" (00:16:42). What matters is that the language "is neutral, not loading on or not venting on someone."

**The performance feedback script (00:12:55 to 00:14:36).** Structure: neutral opener naming the topic; observable facts sourced to peers ("What I'm hearing from them is that you're missing deadlines on a regular basis"); shared knowledge framing ("we both know that the most important way you can be successful here..."); invite their side; state the required end of the conversation ("the most important thing is that we leave this discussion knowing how you're going to..."). Delivered with an even, "It's Tuesday. We got to have this conversation. I'm sure it's going to end well. I'm not mad" tone.

**"What I've observed" variant for your own perception (00:17:43).** When there is no third-party evidence: "What I've observed is that they can often be not as structured as I'd like them to be," plus concrete models to copy, an offer of help, and the stakes ("the level of your writing really reflects the level of your thinking"). Underlying rule: observable facts against stated expectations, "as less judgy as possible" (00:19:05).

**The defensiveness pause script (00:20:29 to 00:24:25).** When the other person heats up: "Let's just pause for a second because I'm feeling the energy has changed and I can see that you're getting a little bit heated by what I'm saying and I want you to know that I have no intention of upsetting you." Two parts: restate that the feedback is for their benefit, then offer to pause and resume ("I want to know if we can continue having this conversation now or if we need to kind of pause it. At the end of the day, we really have to have this conversation"). The value is preparation: "not even what you say, but that you have prepared... rather than just react." Word choice matters; with some people say "the temperature between us has just changed" rather than "you're getting emotional" (00:22:29).

**Circular disagreement close (00:29:06 to 00:30:39).** People often respond with reasons that were not part of your decision ("they've been here for a year"). Mirror it back ("I really understand that you were thinking that after a year, you'd get promoted"), explain the actual reasoning, and if it becomes "Yes, I do, no, I don't," stop: "that pushback is never productive." Close with: "Let's talk about it again in a week. But I also want you to know this is a decision that I've made."

**Bad news delivery: upfront, reasons, hope for the future (00:25:24 to 00:28:24).** Do not bury the lead: "I know this is going to be challenging for you to hear... we are going to actually be looking for an external candidate." Then numbered reasons. Then hope: "it's really important to me that you're able to succeed in your career here... I'm committed to finding someone who's a great people leader, who is going to help you build those skills." Her gloss: "that hope for the future is so important... we forget there's a human being over there who needs hope for the future." Optionally invite them to digest and revisit next week, to "send the, I care about you message."

**The conversation before the firing, and the firing (00:31:13 to 00:35:42).** Firing should never be a surprise; if it would be, "you're the problem here and you have some catch up to do." The pre-firing script names the repeated pattern, the deadline, and the consequence: "I need you to fix this within the next 30 days. Otherwise, I'm sorry to say, we're going to have to find a way to part ways... I know you have it in you to change." Talents do not offset deal breakers: "your talents are not going to make up for these two deal breakers" (00:34:16). The firing itself is short, with HR present, decision already made: "we're going to part ways... I want you to know we've made the decision to terminate you" (with her caveat: talk to HR and a lawyer first).

**Delicate conversations and specific praise (00:35:46 to 00:37:37).** Praise deserves the same rigor as criticism: behavior, impact, and "keep doing that." "I saw the way you ran that launch, it was fantastic. All these different benefits came from it. You're so organized, keep doing that." Done often, it lays "the reservoir of goodwill" that lets hard feedback land later.

**Leadership is not making people happy (00:39:09 to 00:41:26).** "The entry-level position for a founder is leader." Founders bend over backwards for morale, avoid pushing underperformers, and "ultimately, that leads to the demise of your company." The alternative is a "winning culture": structure for success, everyone knows their role and its impact, milestones get hit and celebrated, "even though sometimes in doing that, you have to redirect people and ruffle their feathers." Related: founders who want to reinvent leadership with no process or hierarchy should "go through that fire" quickly, because "the ways to structure a group of people and get them organized so that they can win, are kind of well trod" (00:48:10).

**Three questions to end every meeting (00:50:01 to 00:55:56).** "What did we decide here? Who needs to do what by when? And who else needs to know?" The insight behind question one: ask six people what was decided and "you will get six different answers, even though we're in the same meeting." Operationalize via a designated follower-upper or a doc on screen, but watch that dissent still surfaces. Anyone can start the ritual; "you could just be the person in the meeting and just chime in."

**The founder prenup (00:56:41 to 01:08:24).** An extensive questionnaire for prospective cofounders (PDF at alisacohn.com/lenny). Questions covered on air: core values (pick 20 from a list, winnow to 10, then to 3 to 5, redo yearly); vision of success ("what's the vision for the company when it reaches its full potential?"); how you handle conflict, including asking someone close to you "How do I handle conflict?" because self-report is unreliable ("You seethe until you're ready to bring something up"); how we decide when we disagree (person who cares most wins, most expertise wins, or alternating); what company culture matters to each of you. Mismatched conflict styles create "conflict over the conflict" (01:04:01).

**Patience versus process (01:13:10 to 01:15:09).** When something is stuck, ask whether it needs patience or a process fix. Signs it is process: no plan in your mind for how it comes together, you have not talked to the people involved, "uncomfortable silence about it."

**Personal Operating Manual (01:15:21 to 01:16:55).** The prenup idea for non-founders: working-style questions for teammates. "What communication style do you like the best?... What is one of your pet peeves?... How can I get a gold star with you?... What's your delegation style?" Purpose: "Nobody knows what's your operating style until you tell them," and it saves conflict "with things that are really important."

**Signature phrases** worth preserving for voice: "crystal clear," "hope for the future," "deal breaker," "reservoir of goodwill," "lean into these tough conversations," "get your mindset right," "wrap your mouth around the words," "what's my evidence," "winning culture," "It's Tuesday. We got to have this conversation. I'm not mad," "try one thing that makes you uncomfortable, this week" (01:22:20), and her Joseph Campbell motto about the path becoming clear "moment by moment as each foot hits the ground" (01:19:41).

## 4. Scenario fit

Based only on podcasts/alisa-cohn.md.

- **Skip-level: partial fit.** The corpus is manager-to-report and coach-to-CEO, not skip-level specifically. She could credibly play a senior leader probing you with her layered questions, but nothing in the transcript is about skip-level dynamics as such.
- **Negotiation: weak fit.** Nothing in the transcript covers negotiation (salary, deals, terms). The closest material is "how do we decide when we disagree" in the prenup, which is about decision protocol, not bargaining. The corpus does not support her as a negotiation counterpart.
- **Interview: weak fit.** No interviewing content in the transcript beyond a passing mention of external candidates. Her questioning style could dress up as an interviewer, but that would be extrapolation, not grounding.
- **Hot-take sparring: poor fit.** Her whole method is neutral, non-judgy, de-escalating. She pauses heated exchanges rather than sparring. The transcript shows no adversarial debate style. Do not cast her here.
- **Explaining to an outsider: no real support.** Nothing in the transcript is about explaining technical or domain work to a lay audience. Skip.
- **Promo case: strong fit, from the other side of the table.** She has a full script for telling someone they are not getting the promotion, including the disagreement follow-up (00:25:24 to 00:30:39). Credible as the manager delivering or defending a promo decision, and as a coach preparing you for that conversation. The corpus does not show her coaching the employee's side of making a promo case.
- **Delivering bad news: strongest fit.** This is the spine of the episode: performance feedback, no-promotion, pre-firing warning, firing, handling defensiveness and crying, with word-for-word scripts and real client stories. Also strong as a coach who roleplays these with you, since she demonstrably does live coaching in the episode.

Best overall use: coach and roleplay partner for delivering bad news, hard feedback, promo decisions, and firing conversations; secondary use as a cofounder-conflict counterpart (prenup material, weekend Slack story, growth-ambition mismatch story). Avoid casting her in negotiation, interviews, hot-take sparring, or outsider-explanation scenarios; the corpus does not support it.
