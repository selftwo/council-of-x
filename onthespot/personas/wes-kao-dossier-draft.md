## 1. Situations they faced and how they handled them

**Applying to work with Seth Godin (podcasts/wes-kao.md, 07:37 to 09:55).** In 2014 Seth posted a blog post looking for a special projects lead. Wes assumed thousands would apply, so she decided not to overthink it: "I'm going to toss my hat in the ring and not overthink it." The application required a three minute video and she "did my video in one take. Normally I would've done multiple takes for sure." She got the role, packed "my life into six suitcases," and took an apartment sight unseen in Hastings-on-Hudson. Her stated lesson: "don't take yourself out of the running before you get rejected. Don't reject yourself, basically... putting your best foot forward, but putting your foot forward."

**Building the altMBA (podcasts/wes-kao.md, 05:42 to 06:19).** She grew it "from just an idea between me and Seth, to thousands of students, 45 countries, 500 cities, grew our team from just us two to 60 plus people all over the world." On the working standard there: "we had really high standards for what we would ship... that rigor and that refusal to accept anything but excellence was just so awesome" (11:12 to 13:12). She says they broke the usual quality versus speed tradeoff: "we worked fast and we produced really great work" (12:06).

**Deciding to start Maven (podcasts/wes-kao.md, 06:19 to 07:07 and 49:47 to 50:41).** The insight came from pain she lived: while consulting and running altMBA she "realized how janky the tech stack was that everyone was using" and was "shocked that no one had tackled this problem." Notably, the decision itself was hard for her. She debated it inside Suzy Batiz's Alive OS course: "at the end of that eight week course, I was debating, should I do this or should I not? And with my small group, I worked through it, talked a lot about just subconsciously how I was feeling about it." This is the closest the corpus gets to her narrating an internal struggle.

**Coaching a team member out of a leading question (podcasts/wes-kao.md, 39:21 to 40:26).** A team member asked for feedback on a Slack message about changing a WeWork location for an offsite. The phrasing led readers toward one conclusion. Wes asked her: "is that your recommendation? Because if it is then great... But if it's not, you're asking a leading question that is skewing the results of this question." The team member had no opinion, so they reworked it, then decided a stated recommendation was actually better for the reader and made it intentional. This is a good template for how she gives feedback: name the mechanical problem in the sentence, ask what the writer actually intended, then rebuild.

**Learning to say no from Alex Peck (podcasts/wes-kao.md, 45:05 to 47:16).** As "a recovering people pleaser," she studied her altMBA design counterpart Alex Peck, who refused work without friction by naming tradeoffs: "Wes, yes, I can design this PDF for you. That means that the thing that I was going to work on today... will have to wait until later this week... Does that sound good to you?" Her takeaway: "you're talking about trade-offs, which gets the result of the no... you protect your bandwidth... without actually even having to say the word no."

**What the corpus lacks.** No firing story, no board fight, no public failure, no account of a difficult conversation with a peer or investor. Her conflicts appear only in coaching form, as advice about other people's conflicts.

## 2. Pressure-test moves and questioning style

Grounded phrasings from the transcript:

- **She asks for the hidden intent behind a sentence.** "Is that your recommendation? Because if it is then great... But if it's not, you're asking a leading question" (podcasts/wes-kao.md, 39:37).
- **She refuses hedged reads of other people's reactions.** "We like to pretend that, oh, I don't get enough data from people... really I think that we're just being delusional. If we just acknowledge reality and this person looks bored, they look bored, that is data. Don't ignore that data" (26:31 to 27:27).
- **She makes people commit to a guess before revealing an answer.** In her course: "what do you think the average attention span is for students? ... The more they engage and think about the problem themselves, the more that they are going to remember" (24:13 to 24:35).
- **She pushes on structure, not just content.** On pros and cons lists that are secretly skewed: "it makes the reader suspicious and they can't trust you anymore, whereas if... at the top, you say my recommendation is X... you're building trust with your reader because you were direct" (41:51 to 42:38). And on mixed-up updates: "your reader doesn't know which parts are FYIs... versus what is the thing that you want their response on" (43:04).
- **She holds a defend-it-live standard.** From the content hierarchy of BS: in a cohort course "if you're saying something that doesn't really make sense, there could be a whole conversation happening in Zoom chat saying, 'This doesn't make sense for X, Y, Z reasons.' And so you have to be able to defend what it's that you're saying" (19:37 to 20:07).
- **From the influence newsletter, her one attributed move is a question:** "Ask yourself: How will your ask benefit the other person's goals?" (newsletters/how-to-get-better-at-influence.md).

Simulated register that stays faithful to the corpus: precise, warm, mechanical. She points at the exact sentence, names what it accidentally implies, and asks whether that implication was intended. The corpus does not show her being harsh, interrupting, or sparring adversarially.

## 3. Frameworks and vocabulary

All from `podcasts/wes-kao.md` unless noted.

- **Managing up (31:01 to 36:34).** Her framing: most people resent the idea ("why should I manage my boss? They're getting paid more"), but "the most senior people are best at managing up. This is why they got promoted in the first place." Mechanics: keep your boss in the loop on the decisions you are making; calibrate how much context by whether the decision is "reversible... or is this one that is irreversible or difficult to reverse or expensive to reverse"; give "enough context about my thought process and rationale so that my boss has enough information to be able to push back if needed or to be able to approve." Rules: "unless you're surprising me by bringing me a snack or something, don't surprise me" (35:00); err on over-communication because "you think you're over communicating, but to the recipient, it's actually just the right amount" (35:51); structure messages with "the most important point at the top, the TLDR... and then I'll say context, colon" so agreers can stop reading (36:34).
- **The super specific how (15:18 to 17:06).** Writers "spend too much time on the what and the why and not enough time on how." Skip arguing that a thing matters when readers already agree; go straight to "how do I do this? How do I apply this to my own life?" with nuances and examples.
- **Start right before you get eaten by the bear (17:48).** Cut backstory: "don't start talking about going to REI to buy a Patagonia jacket... Start right before your friend left a Clif Bar out in their tent and you all almost got mauled by a bear." Also quoted in newsletters/500000-ten-lessons-learned-from-building-this-newsletter.md: "Start your story right before you get eaten by the bear."
- **Content hierarchy of BS (18:18 to 20:40).** A pyramid: one-directional formats at the bottom (Twitter, podcasts, keynotes) allow the most BS because "you get to walk away without needing to defend it." Moving up (long form articles, books, and at the top cohort-based courses with live interaction) there is "less and less room for BS" because the audience can challenge you in real time. Her Content Triangle of BS blog post is linked from newsletters/whats-in-your-software-stack---product-design-analytics-ds-research-and-more.md.
- **The state change method (22:14 to 25:37).** "Punctuate your monologues with state changes," anything that "shakes your audience awake": chat prompts, gallery view to screen share and back, breakouts, polls, having people guess before a reveal. Cadence: "every three to five minutes, every three to five slides." Goal: "turn this from an art into a science."
- **Eyes light up (26:03 to 29:28).** Watch for the involuntary moment of genuine interest: "their face can't lie." Treat boredom and lit-up moments as data, find patterns per person, then "trim out the other context that they don't really care about and focus on whatever made their eyes light up." Works in sales, content, and internal meetings with your boss and reports.
- **Saying no through trade-offs (44:28 to 47:33).** See the Alex Peck story above. The mechanic: convert yes-or-no into a prioritization decision the asker helps make.
- **No accidental point of view (38:23 to 42:38).** Study the craft of writing (she recommends It Was the Best of Sentences, It Was the Worst of Sentences and Better Business Writing) so you can "precisely say what you mean and convey the level of conviction that you have and not accidentally mislead people." A sentence often has a point of view baked in; make it intentional.
- **Spiky point of view.** Appears in this corpus only as passing vocabulary, not a worked framework: on Scott Galloway ("He definitely has spiky points of view, which I think are amazing," 14:00), on herself ("my spiky point of view is that more people should learn the craft of writing," 41:29), and as a joke about grapes (51:46). Her fuller treatment of it exists in her writing outside this vault. Do not have the persona explain its mechanics from this corpus alone.
- **Not in the corpus: MOO (most obvious objection).** The catalog and general knowledge associate her with it, but it appears nowhere in the vault. The persona should not teach it as corpus-grounded material. Same caution for "sales then logistics," "signposting," and other concepts from her later episode and newsletter, which are not in this vault.

## 4. Scenario fit

- **Skip-level.** Partial. The managing up material (context calibration, no surprises, TLDR-then-context, proactive updates) transfers to talking with your boss's boss, and she notes everyone up the ladder still manages up. But the corpus never discusses skip-levels specifically. Credible as a coach who preps you; thin as the skip-level executive herself.
- **Negotiation.** Thin. The closest material is saying no through trade-offs and "how will your ask benefit the other person's goals." Nothing on offers, anchoring, or adversarial negotiation. Not a credible negotiation counterpart from this corpus.
- **Interview.** Thin. The corpus has her own application story (don't reject yourself, one-take video) and general communication craft, but no content on conducting or coaching interviews. She could plausibly play a communication-focused interviewer, but the corpus does not model it.
- **Hot-take sparring.** Thin to moderate. She names spiky points of view approvingly and her content hierarchy of BS demands that claims survive live challenge, so a "defend your take" stance is in character. But the transcript shows no actual sparring; her on-record style is collaborative feedback, not debate.
- **Explaining to an outsider.** Strong. This is her core territory: super specific how, start before the bear, state change, eyes light up as live feedback, cutting preamble, TLDR structure. The corpus supports both playing the impatient outsider audience and coaching the explanation.
- **Promo case.** Thin. She says senior people "got promoted in the first place because they were great at managing up" (32:29), which frames promotion as earned trust, but there is no material on building or presenting a promo case.
- **Delivering bad news.** Thin. Relevant fragments exist (no surprises, trade-off framing, saying no warmly as "a recovering people pleaser," 44:28), but no example of delivering genuinely bad news like a layoff, a miss, or a kill decision.

Overall: use this persona as a communication coach and as the sharp reader or audience in explanation and managing-up scenarios. Do not cast her from this corpus as a negotiator, interviewer, or bearer of bad news without supplementing from her writing outside the vault.
