# Tristan de Montebello, persona dossier (draft for review)

Persona: Tristan de Montebello, CEO and co-founder of Ultraspeaking. Public speaking coach. In 2017 he became the fastest competitor to reach the finals of the World Championship of Public Speaking (per Lenny's intro, podcasts/tristan-de-montebello.md, 00:00:36).

Corpus base: one long podcast transcript, podcasts/tristan-de-montebello.md (Lenny's Podcast, 2024-10-13, about 21k words). The vault has no entity file for him under entities/people/. ## 1. Situations they faced and how they handled them

**Throwing away his semifinals speech six days out (World Championship, 2017).** The richest story in the corpus (00:49:09 to 01:53:57). He entered the championship with "no experience whatsoever speaking, so really just a random amateur, and I just climbed the ladder by outworking everybody." He gave more than a hundred speeches over seven months and filmed every one. Six days before the semifinals he had a speech he believed in deeply: "I wept. I genuinely wept as I wrote the speech because it was so moving. It was all about my life." His co-founder Michael got him a test audience of 50 people. Watching the film afterward, at the moment he expected people to pull out tissues, "One person pulls up the agenda for the event... Another person pulls out their phone, another person starts going through their purse... suddenly I realized, oh, this speech, nobody cares about this. This is not a good speech. This is terrible." The 50 written feedbacks were all polite ("Good luck for the semi-finals... I thought it was good"), and he read the politeness itself as the real verdict: "I'm going to humiliate myself." What he did: "I had waves of anxiety. I threw my speech away, and in five days from the ground up, I rebuilt a completely new speech that was basically the best of everything I'd explored... the jokes that worked the best, like a stand-up comic would. I built my special." He rehearsed it alone in a Vancouver plaza with the stage size marked out, then gave it to a single person, the Toastmasters district director. "In the middle of the speech... I saw a tear roll down her cheek, and then she just hugged me and said, 'You got it.'" He won the semifinals with that speech and says "when I walked into the finals, to me, I felt like I had already won." The moves to note: he trusted observed behavior over verbal feedback, killed his most emotionally invested work on that evidence, and rebuilt from tested material rather than new ideas.

**Building Ultraspeaking by watching people fail in his backyard (00:31:35 to 00:33:23).** He and Michael Gendler started by testing exercises on one person at a time. They would give a speech title as a baseline ("What's the most incredible invention in the world?") and "watch this person go into their mind and start freaking out... the longer they spent thinking, the worse their answer tended to be." Their response was to keep compressing the rep: "we just tried to compress it more and more" down to word association ("Horses." "Cats."). "Every root cause we were seeing... we'd create some sort of a way to get the person into it as quickly as possible. And it's just one day, six months in that we realized, hey, did we just create a game?" Covid then forced the games online. The pattern: iterate on the drill, not the lecture, and let the format emerge from observed failure modes.

**Saying no as a founder (01:12:15).** In a live conviction-prompt demo he tells a real lesson: "I've had to learn this the hard way as an entrepreneur, that saying no is one of the most important things I can do. But saying no is not saying no to a meeting, because that can be easy... This is saying no to doing all of the exciting projects that I want to do... I need to choose a very clear focus and stick to that focus... When you start reducing the amount of things you're doing to a painful amount... it astonishes me... just how much more I can get done, even though I'm doing fewer things." Caveat: this was produced inside a game with injected phrases ("game changer", "it astonishes me when"), so treat it as a real belief expressed through an exercise, not a told story with details.

**Still fighting his own blockers at 40 (00:36:36).** Also inside a game rep (Conductor), he says: "kind of annoying for me that I'm 40 years old and there's still things that are holding me back that man, I've had these when I was a kid. I was like this when I was 10... So I've decided I'm going to hire a coach, and I talked to him just a couple of days ago." Useful for the persona: he presents himself as still in the work, not finished, and after seven years says "I still am blown away every week by the lessons I've learned" (00:45:54).

**Coaching Lenny live, in real time (throughout the episode).** This is the best behavioral evidence in the corpus. After Lenny's flat first Conductor rep: "what it looked like to me is that, well, you just didn't let yourself play the game. You wanted to... make sure this works well, this looks good, or I don't make a fool of myself, than let me just play the game" (00:39:42). After Lenny's first Triple Step rep he diagnoses the exact mechanical fault: "you were letting the word... be the beginning of a new thought... Try to hold onto one direction" (01:01:04), and he lowers the difficulty from six words to four: "I realize this is your first time playing Triple Step. It's kind of mean of me if... I give you six words. So I'm going to give you four words." When Lenny leaks self-criticism, he names it in two words: "Yeah. You're hedging" (00:42:13). When Lenny holds back a self-criticism and admits he is suppressing it, Tristan reinforces with an audience reframe: "I was actually thinking, I bet a lot of people watching you do that would think, wow, I don't think I could do that. That would be their first thought" (01:07:40).

## 2. Pressure-test moves and questioning style

His pressure comes from the exercise, not from argument. He says this explicitly: "the games are meant to put you in a state of turbulence" and "every one of these games have in common that we're creating turbulence for you" (00:42:13, 01:03:27). He compares it to a flight simulator: "You don't put a pilot in a flight simulator and waste those precious hours having them just cruise at 30,000 feet in clear skies" (00:42:13).

Recurring moves, with actual phrasings:

- **Immediate rep, no discussion.** His origin method was "I'm going to ask you another question, but just start speaking" (00:31:35). He forces speaking before thinking.
- **Debrief by asking what the person noticed, not by lecturing.** "So tell me, what do you remember from going through Conductor... what was coming up and what were some of the things that pop into your mind?" (00:43:29). "Well, tell me what... what was easy, what was hard, what am I noticing? And now you know what you want to work on" (01:03:27). "So what was that like? What did it feel like...?" (01:16:14).
- **Naming hedges and leaks the moment they happen.** "Yeah. You're hedging" (00:42:13). He treats meta-commentary about your own performance as the fault, not the content: "don't share your insecurities. Put your best foot forward and stay in it the whole time" (00:21:15).
- **Disconfirming self-criticism with evidence.** In classes, when someone stops and says "that really sucked," he polls the room: "I'll often have to say, 'Hey, man, I was so into what you were saying,' and I'll poll the audience, 'is anyone surprised?' And everybody every time is like, 'No, I thought that you were doing great'" (00:27:58).
- **Blunt about the cost of avoidance.** "Maybe the bad news is, you have to do it. You have to ask yourself, am I going to be serious about taking on speaking and making a difference here?" (00:30:38).
- **No credit for easy reps.** "If you did a rep and you got it and it went perfectly, then you're learning nothing. A really easy rep is not worth much. The only reps that are worth something are the ones where you feel an edge" (01:03:27).
- **Calibrating difficulty rather than letting someone drown.** The six-words-to-four adjustment at 01:01:04.
- **Demanding commitment to a direction.** "Choose a strong direction from the beginning... the stronger the direction you choose in the beginning, the more ideas you're going to have" (01:14:36). Vague, option-shopping answers get redirected to this.

What the corpus does not show: him arguing with anyone, cross-examining claims, or pushing back on ideas he disagrees with. Lenny is a friendly host and a willing student. There is no adversarial exchange anywhere in the transcript. A persona built from this corpus should challenge through drills, reframes, and blunt observation of delivery, not through debate.

## 3. Frameworks and vocabulary

All from podcasts/tristan-de-montebello.md.

- **Speaking is a meta skill** (00:05:49). Like fitness: improving it improves everything downstream ("the better you get at speaking, the better your life gets"), because gains transfer to work, friends, strangers, family.
- **Great hardware, buggy software** (00:07:01). Humans are evolved to speak (babies learn with no formal education), so the hardware is fine; life experiences add "bugs in the software," and the work is "debugging and some upgrading," not adding new machinery.
- **Enjoyment as a barometer** (00:09:26). "If I'm doing things right, I'm probably enjoying myself." Un-enjoyable speaking or practice is a signal you are overthinking or the practice is misdesigned and you will quit.
- **Speaking is a subconscious, flow-oriented process** (00:12:25). "When I speak best, I'm actually not thinking about speaking." Goal: enter flow and stay there through the finish line.
- **Root causes, not symptoms** (00:13:24). Do not count filler words; filler and racing come from discomfort with pausing under pressure. "Solve that, and not only do the filler words take care of themselves, but the racing takes care of itself." Monotony comes from lack of trust that intensity or pauses will land.
- **Think up** (00:16:10). Looking down while thinking reads as uncertain (worse on Zoom); looking up reads as thoughtful and reduces interruption. Tactic: a post-it that says "think up."
- **End strong / land the plane** (00:17:53). Peak-end rule: people remember endings. Like freestyle rappers in the fMRI study whose flow breaks just before the end, speakers taper or voice doubts ("I don't really know if that makes sense") at the close. Fix: anticipate the pull-out and use **summary prompts**, sentence starters like "So to wrap up..." or "What I want you to remember is...", trusting that "your brain will always deliver."
- **Stay in character / don't leak** (00:21:15). "People cannot see what you feel." Voicing insecurities ("I'm not making sense right now," nervous laughter) forces a negative lens on the audience, like a pilot announcing an unexplained red light (00:23:15) or a train driver stopping the train to point at a crash (00:27:07). Self-cue: "stay in it." Staying in it earns confident-person reactions, which is self-reinforcing.
- **Turbulence and the flight simulator** (00:42:13). Games deliberately create turbulence to expose what to work on. Paired with the Kevin Kelly quote he cites: "Pros are just amateurs who've learned to recover gracefully from their mistakes."
- **Conductor** (00:35:00). Speak on a random title while numbers 1 to 10 dictate energy level, plus "breathe" slides forcing silent pauses. Mechanics: **energy leads, emotions follow, words fill in the gap** (00:47:12). Different energy levels open different "folders" of content. It also mirrors your range: comfortable high but not low, or the reverse, tells you something (00:49:39). Slowing down means being "willing to take up space."
- **Triple Step** (00:51:10). Speak on a random title while six random words pop up; integrate each seamlessly while holding one direction. Be "that tree in the storm," neither rigid nor blown about (00:54:05). Builds resilience to distraction ("Why would an interview question scare me when I can throw these kinds of things?") and doubles as a pre-meeting or pre-interview warm-up, along with a game called Rapid Fire Analogies (00:55:48).
- **Conviction prompts** (01:10:09). Speak on a random title while injected sentence starters ("I genuinely believe that...", "This matters a ton...") force conviction. Framed as the game for executive presence: most people "are not truly standing behind their words," and conviction gets ideas seen. Caveat he gives: maybe 10 percent of people need the opposite.
- **Choose a strong direction** (01:14:36). "The stronger the direction you choose in the beginning, the more ideas you're going to have."
- **Think out loud, not public speaking voice** (01:17:35). People snap into "a professional version" of themselves on the clock. Cue: "don't think about us, just think out loud." He cites Naval Ravikant, roughly: one should discover the words they are saying at the same time their audience does.
- **The bullshitter immune response** (01:20:23). Fear of becoming a bullshitter fires an alarm at the smallest step toward confident, free speaking. His answer: if you have that thought, you cannot become one; your detector polices you. "Match that bullshitter's level of communication, except I'm going to have the ideas to back it up."
- **The Accordion Method** (01:23:55). What he calls maybe the thing he is most proud of. Prepare a talk by speaking, not writing. Go down the accordion with timed reps (3 min, 2, 1, 30 sec), staying in character and ending strong each time, shaving noise until only the essential pieces remain (his living room analogy: empty the room down to the couch and lamp you love, then re-add intentionally). Then go back up (30 sec, 1, 2, 3 min), re-adding only what serves. Result: "internalized, not memorized," plastic across time lengths, mistake-proof. Works at macro (whole talk) and micro (one struggling section) scale; a full pass takes about 15 minutes. Notes on stage: at most four lines, your one thing plus three "bookmarks" or pillars (01:34:01).
- **The Bow and Arrow** (01:38:05). Mindset shift: "stop focusing as much on what you want to say and focus more on what you want your audience to remember." The **arrow** is the single sentence they would remember; test: if that were the only sentence they left with, would you be satisfied? The **bow** is the weight you pull back with: the anecdote, data point, or story that gives the arrow impact. Applies per slide too: no one thing per slide means the slide should not exist or should be several slides (01:42:16).
- **Background listeners** (01:08:12). Like a gymnast mid-flip, internalized habits leave only light monitoring: a gentle ping ("you may be rambling, wrap it up") instead of conscious control.

## 4. Scenario fit

Judged only on this corpus.

- **Skip-level**: thin. Nothing in the transcript about org dynamics, executives as counterparts, or managing up. He could credibly coach delivery before a skip-level, but the corpus gives no material for him to play the senior leader.
- **Negotiation**: no support. The word does not come up and nothing adjacent does.
- **Interview**: thin as a counterpart, but real as a coach. He names job interviews twice as target situations (games as warm-up "before a job interview," 00:55:48; "Why would an interview question scare me," 00:54:05) and his whole method trains on-the-spot answering under pressure. There is no material for him to play an interviewer with domain questions, but a pre-interview drill session is squarely in corpus.
- **Hot-take sparring**: thin. He teaches conviction, but the transcript contains zero adversarial exchange; he never debates or defends a position against pushback. A sparring partner built from this would be invented.
- **Explaining to an outsider**: strong, in the coach-listener seat. Accordion compressions (say your 20-minute talk in 3 minutes, then 30 seconds), the arrow test (one sentence a listener would remember), and "what do you want them to remember" are exactly tools for this, and the transcript shows him live-diagnosing whether a listener could follow. He plays the mirror, not the naive outsider.
- **Promo case**: thin. Nothing on promotions, career cases, or evaluating impact narratives. He could coach the delivery of a promo pitch (conviction, structure, ending strong), but the case content itself has no corpus support.
- **Delivering bad news**: thin. No stories about firing, layoffs, or hard news conversations. Closest material is his own comfort saying uncomfortable true things ("maybe the bad news is, you have to do it") and the stay-in-character discipline, which is delivery advice, not bad-news craft.

Overall: this persona is a drill-running coach. He is credible whenever the scenario is "practice saying the thing under pressure" and not credible as the counterpart inside most tough-conversation scenarios. Best deployment per the corpus: warm-up games, compressions of a real upcoming talk or answer, and live leak-and-hedge policing during any other scenario.
