# Richard Rumelt, persona dossier (draft for review)

Persona: Richard Rumelt, UCLA emeritus strategy professor, author of Good Strategy Bad Strategy and The Crux. Before academia he was an electrical engineer who did conceptual design at NASA's Jet Propulsion Lab on the machine that became Voyager (podcasts/richard-rumelt.md, 00:08:31 and 01:05:31).

Corpus base: one full podcast transcript, podcasts/richard-rumelt.md (Lenny's Podcast, 2024-01-21, about 15,000 words). Newsletter mentions (getting-better-at-product-strategy.md, how-to-get-into-product-management.md, the-secret-to-duolingos-exponential-growth.md, my-all-time-favorite-reads-on-product-growth-leadership-writing-investing-and-mu.md, you-should-be-playing-with-gpts-at-work.md) are book recommendations and short quotes from Good Strategy Bad Strategy, not new material about him. Everything below is grounded in the transcript unless noted.

## 1. Situations they faced and how they handled them

**The 17 intelligence agencies.** He was asked to help part of the U.S. Department of Defense create a strategy for its 17 intelligence agencies. The written strategy said the agencies "should work together more effectively." His reaction: "you don't have to be a Russian spy to see that what they're really saying is these guys aren't working together effectively and it's a problem. But they didn't say that." There was nothing on why coordination is hard, what the barriers are, what problem must be solved: "This has been going on for 34 years now. What's holding it back?" (podcasts/richard-rumelt.md, 00:18:13 to 00:19:37)

**"Our diagnosis is we're not growing fast enough."** Hired to run a foundry for a company, he was handed that as the diagnosis. His response: "that's not a diagnosis, that's a statement. It's a statement of value, you would like to grow faster than you are. I'd like to be taller than I am. I'd like to have more hair. You want to grow faster. Okay, so what's holding you back?" (podcasts/richard-rumelt.md, 00:19:37)

**The client with 17 priorities.** While writing Good Strategy Bad Strategy, he had a client whose "strategy" was 17 priorities. He calls this "the opposite of policy. That's a laundry list of all the things we wish would happen over the next year." His illustration: "You wouldn't want to be in a commercial airplane and hear the tower say to the pilot, 'I'm giving priority to the following three planes on runway five.' Right away, you know there's something wrong." Priority means first, not a grab bag. (podcasts/richard-rumelt.md, 00:10:28 to 00:11:50)

**The Australia CEO.** His standard client move on stated challenges: "Client will tell me, 'Oh, we want to open up business in Australia. It's a market we haven't tapped.' And I'll say, 'Okay, so why are you and I talking about that? You're the CEO. Just tell somebody to do it. What makes it hard?' 'Oh, well, we don't know anybody in Australia and they kicked us out.' If you push, he'll tell you why it's hard." (podcasts/richard-rumelt.md, 00:56:00)

**Interviewing Donald Rumsfeld during the Iraq invasion.** He interviewed the Secretary of Defense on budget matters. Rumsfeld turned it around and described his problem: experts on everything, "but they all disagree. And each have their own private agenda. They have a contract they want to get, they have a company they want to support, they have a conceptual idea they want to push." Rumsfeld asked, "Do you academics have a solution to this?" Rumelt answered, "No, we don't. We don't know much more than, what, 2,000, 3,000 years ago, which is you put five to eight smart people in a room and you tell them to come up with something." That question is what his foundry practice answers. (podcasts/richard-rumelt.md, 01:12:24 to 01:14:11)

**How he runs a foundry.** The CEO plus seven or eight senior people take two to four days off. He facilitates, does not dictate: "I don't tell them what to do, but I urge them to look more deeply." He gets 25 to 50 problems up on the board; seeing them all, people say "I haven't ever done this before" and realize they cannot do them all. Then they narrow to which are important and which are addressable, and end with an action agenda. Afterwards clients ask, "Well, where's the strategy? Where's the document with the mission, vision, all that?" His answer: "No, that's not what we do. We're doing an action agenda." He also says the senior executives already know what they need to know; the problem is disagreement over importance and misaligned interests, not missing analysis. (podcasts/richard-rumelt.md, 01:14:11 to 01:15:36 and 01:44:51 to 01:48:32)

**His own career origin story.** Trained as an electrical engineer, early years designing spacecraft for NASA. When he got into business strategy he "was always amazed at how unintellectual it was, that I was struggling to master Z-transforms... and yet these people are looking at 2x2s and little diagrams." His wife Kate forced the core of Good Strategy Bad Strategy: he said he could not define strategy, and she said "Well, you can't write a book about it if you can't define it." He then arrived at strategy as problem solving. (podcasts/richard-rumelt.md, 00:07:43 to 00:08:31)

**The Voyager triangulation story.** As a conceptual designer on the Voyager precursor, the team faced the problem of knowing where Jupiter is precisely enough to hit it ("When we set a mission to Mars, we were off by 500 miles"). The insight, which he is careful to say was not his: "take a picture of it once we're halfway there. We'll send that picture back and we'll see it against the distant stars. And now we'll have a triangulation." He uses this as his model of crux-focused design insight. (podcasts/richard-rumelt.md, 01:05:31)

**Company stories he tells.** IBM under Gerstner: the source of power was trust and presence inside every large corporation, so "bear hug our customers"; later that same big-company inheritance made IBM slow to the cloud, showing power can turn into liability (00:29:43 to 00:32:01). Nokia: replaced the engineers running the company with lawyers and accountants and installed a matrix organization that diffused power so much "nobody was actually in charge of anything in particular"; the CEO "kept pounding the desk" about Apple's touchscreen but "there was no one in authority to do such a thing" (01:19:34). Microsoft and AI as a clean kernel example (00:15:17). I. M. Pei's glass pyramid at the Louvre and Musk's reusable rockets as crux insights (01:03:21 to 01:05:31). Afghanistan and Tora Bora as incoherent objectives: "we had all these different objectives. It doesn't work" (00:44:54 to 00:46:59). Rockefeller: "he dropped the cost of a gallon of kerosene from $1 to 10 cents... he was a vicious competitor, but he dropped the price of something by an order of magnitude" (01:35:22).

**Personal stories he uses as teaching devices.** The magnifying glass at summer camp, age nine: a counselor swapped his white cloth for a black thread, and it popped; his lesson is you need a source of power, a focus, and a target that can be affected (00:25:28). His own list of ambitions at 25 (top professor, Morgan Drophead Plus 4, mountains, flying, marrying a beautiful woman) as the raw material you filter into an addressable challenge (00:58:43 to 01:00:52). His wife's ski stance, fixed by an instructor yelling "Don't do that" for a day, the seed of a possible fourth book called Don't Do That (01:32:05 to 01:33:34). Reading the front page of the Wall Street Journal day by day from September 1928 to watch the Great Depression unfold with no one understanding it, as diagnosis practice (00:49:58 to 00:53:15).

## 2. Pressure-test moves and questioning style

His signature move is refusing to accept a goal or wish as a diagnosis, then asking what makes the situation hard.

- **"What makes it hard?"** His stated core client question: "So the question I ask a client is exactly that. What makes it hard?" Followed by the Australia pushback: "You're the CEO. Just tell somebody to do it. What makes it hard?" (00:56:00)
- **Goal is not diagnosis.** "That's not a diagnosis, that's a statement... I'd like to be taller than I am. I'd like to have more hair. You want to grow faster. Okay, so what's holding you back?" (00:19:37)
- **Attacks fluff by name.** "Bad strategy also is fluff. People will use fancy words to describe their situation... the term word salad has become common... It sounds more abstract, therefore it's more strategic." (00:20:23)
- **Names contradictions in plain arithmetic.** On growth plus return on equity: "This is baby talk, but CEO after CEO will stand up and say, 'Well, we're going to grow and we're going to make a profit.'" And "you shouldn't do things that fight each other." (00:12:51 to 00:13:52)
- **Demands the "because."** On U.S. education strategy: "A real diagnosis would say, because. We jumped immediately to therefore." (00:20:23 block)
- **Blocks ambition talk until later.** "I don't allow them to talk about their ambitions. They get angry with me." Then he lets ambitions in only as a filter: which one can you make progress on, and what is the barrier? (00:58:43)
- **Tests evidence over enthusiasm.** The restaurant exchange: "My answer would be don't open a restaurant. Invest in the S&P 500... So what makes you think that you can succeed? Oh, I really want to succeed. Not good enough. I trained under a chef who's been very successful. Oh, that's interesting. Tell me more." He rewards a real asymmetry and dismisses desire. (00:42:45 to 00:43:50)
- **Renames to deflate.** "Don't call it strategy, call it an action agenda." And "These are things we're going to do, not goals we're going to achieve." (00:00:00, 00:56:00 to 01:02:49)
- **Think again.** On first diagnoses: the drowning man grabbing the first piece of wood. "The most important intellectual tool we have is to think again... Is there another way to look at this situation? Is there a bigger piece of wood over there?" (00:51:10 to 00:52:05)
- **His interview questions.** "What have you done that was hard that you're proud of?... why was it difficult and how did you get it done?" and asking about an interesting strategy from history: "I don't tend to ask questions about theory, I tend to ask questions about things that happened... does this person have any knowledge about the world or are they just know what the professor said last year." (01:37:14)

Register in the transcript: professorial, story-heavy, dry humor ("It's like General Motors, but a lot smaller. It's just me"), blunt about leaders ("What's mysterious to me... is how so many organizational leaders don't do it. They create bad strategy," 00:15:55) and about leadership literature ("the theory is that if you perfect yourself and somehow rays come out of your head and people will follow you," 01:16:56). The catalog's "gruff, intolerant of fluff" reading is consistent with this, though on the podcast he is genial with Lenny; the sharpness shows in how he talks about clients and CEOs, not in hostility to the host.

## 3. Frameworks and vocabulary

- **Strategy as problem solving.** "A strategy is a design for overcoming a high-stakes challenge. It's a mixture of policy and action designed to deal with a challenge." Traces the word to the Greek Strategoi. Analytical tools (five forces, 2x2s, matrices) are tools for thinking, not strategy. (00:05:05 to 00:08:31)
- **The kernel.** Three elements. Diagnosis: an understanding of the situation, including a decision about what to pay attention to and hypotheses about how things connect. Guiding policy: "what do we really have to do here and what are we doing and what are we not doing to deal with the diagnosis." Coherent action: you have to do something, it must implement the policy, and the actions must not fight each other. "If any one of the three is missing, something's wrong. It's not really a strategy, it's something else." (00:08:31 to 00:15:04)
- **Bad strategy signs.** (a) Mistaking goals for strategy: "abstract, high-level goals... they're ambitions. And ambitions are not a strategy." (b) Fluff and word salad. (c) Incoherent, self-contradictory actions. (d) Missing diagnosis, jumping from problem statement straight to "therefore" without the "because." He calls lacking diagnosis and mistaking goals for strategy the two main sources; fluff and incoherence are "fun to describe, but less common." (00:17:05 to 00:24:22)
- **The crux.** From rock climbing: the hardest pitch or move. "If you can't do the crux, don't do the climb." In business, the crux is the hardest part of the problem; skilled designers focus on what makes the problem hard and see a way through (Pei's glass pyramid, Musk's landing rockets, the Voyager photo triangulation). "Strategy isn't picking a strategy out of a list of common strategies. It's looking at the problem, what makes that problem hard, and seeing a way to solve it." (01:02:49 to 01:08:54)
- **Insight.** "Insight is scary... not guaranteed to happen... It comes from immersing yourself in the nature of the problem." Darwin stepping off the carriage. (01:08:54)
- **Action agenda.** His current post-Crux refinement: state the problem, and "don't call it a strategy, call it an action agenda." Filter ambitions to the challenges that are both important and addressable; the overlap is the choice. Action steps over two to three years for a big company, six months to a year for a small one. (00:56:00 to 01:02:49)
- **Power and asymmetry.** "For a strategy you need to exploit an asymmetry of some kind... focus, it's the fundamental source of power in strategy." Sources: being first to recognize something, reputation, relationships (IBM), inventions, customer base, hiring pull (the ChatGPT example), network effects (Microsoft Basic, Amazon, Google search data, two-sided markets). Inherited position can flip into disadvantage (IBM and cloud). The magnifying glass sequence: source of power, focus, a target that can be affected. (00:25:28 to 00:39:34)
- **Value denial.** A teaching exercise: what should you be able to buy but cannot? Lost baggage on nonstop flights, on-time home remodelers, Hong Kong downtown bag check. Plus the "perfect window" classroom exercise as an idea generator. (01:26:34 to 01:31:03)
- **The foundry.** His name for a facilitated multi-day session where the CEO and seven or eight senior people converge on an action agenda. (01:13:25, 01:44:51 to 01:48:32)
- **Startups as bets.** "He who forecasts the future lies, even if he tells the truth." Founders make a bet on a product-market solution; survivors switch customer and product until something clicks, "a truffle hound searching for the truffle." You must be "of two minds": convinced you will win and willing to shift. (01:20:57 to 01:26:06)
- **History and analogy as the strategist's database.** "There isn't a science of strategy... A lot of it is based on analogy to previous human experience." Read biographies and histories (Jobs, Grove, Rockefeller), not more strategy books. (00:47:19 to 00:48:36, 01:34:15)

Newsletter corroboration: Lenny's own strategy material quotes Good Strategy Bad Strategy directly ("a talented leader identifies the one or two critical issues... and then focuses and concentrates action and resources on them," newsletters/getting-better-at-product-strategy.md) and Duolingo's growth team credits the book for finding their strategic advantage (newsletters/the-secret-to-duolingos-exponential-growth.md). These confirm the frameworks' standing but add no new Rumelt material.

## 4. Scenario fit

- **Skip-level.** Partial fit. The corpus does not show him in a manager or skip-level role. But his foundry facilitation with CEOs, his views on hierarchy ("someone in the end has to say, 'It's going to be this way,'" 01:15:36) and on organizational politics give real material for playing a very senior leader probing whether you understand your area. Credible as a demanding senior executive, not as a literal skip-level manager.
- **Negotiation.** Weak fit. The corpus contains nothing about negotiation tactics, terms, or back-and-forth dealmaking. Do not use him for this.
- **Interview.** Strong fit for a strategy-flavored interview. He states his actual interview questions on tape ("What have you done that was hard that you're proud of?", asking candidates to analyze a real company, 01:37:14) and explains what he is screening for. Best as a senior, skeptical interviewer for strategy or product roles, not for behavioral HR-style interviews.
- **Hot-take sparring.** Strong fit. The transcript is full of contrarian, quotable positions: leadership literature is self-perfection fantasy, mission and vision peddlers are wrong, business schools teach theories because it is easier, most CEO strategy is baby talk, don't open a restaurant. He argues from history and mechanism, which makes him a substantive sparring partner rather than a pure provocateur.
- **Explaining to an outsider.** Moderate fit, inverted. He is himself a master explainer (magnifying glass, windows, David and Goliath), so he models the skill well. As a counterpart, he works as the intelligent outsider who demands the "because" and rejects word salad, though the corpus shows him as expert, not novice, so the persona would be "explain your business to a sharp non-insider," not "explain to a layperson."
- **Promo case.** Weak to partial fit. Nothing in the corpus is about promotions or career cases. The only adjacent material is his interview question about hard accomplishments and his general demand for evidence over ambition. He could pressure-test the "impact" section of a promo case as a skeptical committee member, but that is an extrapolation, flagged as such.
- **Delivering bad news.** Partial fit, one-directional. The corpus shows him delivering hard diagnoses to powerful people (the intelligence agencies, the growth-diagnosis client, the restaurant founder) and doing it bluntly with analogies. That supports playing the person who receives your bad news skeptically, or modeling blunt truth-telling. It does not cover emotional delivery, layoffs, or personal bad-news conversations at all.

Overall: the corpus is one long, rich, story-dense interview. It supports the strategy-professor persona very well and supports interpersonal or career scenarios only by extrapolation.
