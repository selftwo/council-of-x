# Family E: manager difficult-conversation questions — draft for Ben's review (2026-07-20)

Five concrete questions mined from the Lenny corpus, covering Ben's four named themes: saying no, standing ground on requirements, asking for more (scope, comp, promotion), and getting visibility. Nothing here is a scenario file yet; per the scenario map, files get written after Ben approves.

Citation convention: `podcasts/...` and `newsletters/...` are paths inside `../../sources/lenny-knowledge-graph-vault/`. Persona dossiers are in `../personas/`. Timestamps are transcript speaker headers.

Counterpart casting, stated up front: the fit table (`../personas/fit-summary-draft.md`) says no persona has real negotiation support, and family E is Ben's own manager, not a celebrity. So the counterpart is role-based (Ben's manager) in all five, informed by the cited doctrine. Cohn's scripts supply the manager's calm-but-firm register; Evans supplies the promotion logic; Kao and the newsletters supply what a good managing-up move looks like, which is what the practicer rubric grades Ben against.

## E1. The Friday drop (saying no)

**Situation.** Thursday afternoon, weekly 1:1. Ben is mid-quarter with two committed workstreams, one of which is already at risk on timeline. His manager has just come out of a leadership sync and wants to hand Ben an additional analysis the exec team asked for, due in ten days. The manager is not being unreasonable; the request is real and someone has to do it. The trap is the instant yes (Ben pays later, per newsletters/managing-up.md: "It may feel good to say yes in the moment, but you'll pay for it later") and the flat no (reads as not a team player).

**Manager's opening line.** "Before we get into your update: leadership wants a competitive analysis on the new entrant by the end of next week, and I told them our team would take it. I'd like you to run it. Can you pick it up?"

**Manager's likely pushback moves, grounded:**
- Minimizing the cost: "it's probably a few days of work, tops." Counter-doctrine exists because managers genuinely lack the context: Wes Kao notes managers "will never have as much context as their team" (newsletter.weskao.com/p/15-principles-for-managing-up, outside the vault; in-vault equivalent is her right-sized context doctrine, podcasts/wes-kao.md 33:32).
- Testing whether Ben will name the trade or just absorb it. Kao's Alex Peck story is the model answer the manager should be met with: "Yes, I can design this PDF for you. That means that the thing that I was going to work on today... will have to wait until later this week... Does that sound good to you?" (podcasts/wes-kao.md, 45:27 to 46:21).
- Vague-resource rejection: if Ben says "we're thin on resources," the manager can say "let's just find a way to do both." Tara Seshan's tradeoff doctrine predicts exactly this: weak framing invites "do both"; strong framing names the specific cut and its company-level cost (coaching/02-managing-up-difficult-conversations.md item 26, from newsletters/how-to-communicate-tradeoffs-so-leaders-will-listen.md).

**Strong response (per corpus):** accepts or declines through a priority trade, stated concretely: which named workstream slips, by how much, what that costs the company, then hands the ordering decision back ("do you want me to keep the original priority?", coaching/01 item 45; Lenny's "prioritize and communicate" two-by-two, podcasts/wes-kao.md 47:33). Ethan Evans's card-game variant also passes: name a specific low-value task to hand off while accepting the new one (newsletters/the-magic-loop.md, "discard a 3, add a jack").

**Weak response:** instant unconditional yes; a flat no with no trade named; "I'm pretty slammed" without a specific displaced item; or capitulating after the first "it's only a few days" push. Rubric hooks: P2 (hedged claims), behavior 12 "names the trade" (coaching/coaching-map.md).

## E2. The date versus the requirements (standing ground)

**Situation.** Ben owns a feature with a hard external commitment. Testing has surfaced a real risk: shipping on the committed date means cutting the validation work Ben believes protects the company's reputation with its biggest client. His manager, under pressure from above on the date, wants to descope the checks and ship. Ben thinks that is the wrong gamble and has to hold the position without rank, without capitulating, and without turning it into a loyalty fight.

**Manager's opening line.** "I hear you on the test coverage, but the date went to the client's exec sponsor last month. We're not moving it. What can you cut so we ship on the 30th?"

**Manager's likely pushback moves, grounded:**
- "You're overweighting a tail risk." The corpus's strongest ammunition for Ben is Evans's Wilke scene, where the gamble went wrong: "First, you were wrong. You were wrong to prioritize date over our reputation... Second though, at least you knew you were gambling. If you hadn't known you were gambling, we'd be discussing your departure" (podcasts/ethan-evans-20.md, 00:53:11 to 00:55:53). A strong Ben makes the gamble explicit before it is taken, which is exactly what saved Evans.
- Sturdy close if Ben keeps circling: Cohn's script for repeated disagreement, "You and I have a different point of view. Let's talk about it again in a week. But I also want you to know this is a decision that I've made" (podcasts/alisa-cohn.md, 00:29:06 to 00:30:39). The counterpart should be able to deploy this, forcing Ben to decide whether to disagree-and-commit or escalate cleanly.
- Reframing dissent as obstruction. Evans's own interview bar defends Ben here: he screens FOR people who "needed to disagree with your management... having a bunch of people who just say yes isn't helpful" (podcasts/ethan-evans-20.md, 01:15:03).

**Strong response:** steelmans the date pressure first (coaching/02 item 27, Ami Vora / Bezos "?" doctrine: assume they know something you don't); states the risk as a forced either/or in company terms ("cutting X means risk Y to client Z; I don't think that trade is worth it for the company", Seshan, coaching/02 item 26); names the New York Times headline test (podcasts/ethan-evans-20.md, 00:56:34); if overruled, makes the gamble explicit on the record and commits. Disagreeing via a question also passes: "help me see how shipping without X protects the client commitment" (behavior 10, coaching/coaching-map.md).
**Weak response:** capitulating on the first push; fake-neutral pros and cons hiding a real position (P2 fail, podcasts/wes-kao.md 41:51); escalating to "you're choosing politics over quality" (motive attribution, behavior 14); or restating the same objection three times without moving to decision or escalation (Cohn's "Yes, I do, no, I don't... that pushback is never productive", podcasts/alisa-cohn.md 00:29:06).

## E3. Not this cycle (asking for promotion)

**Situation.** Performance review season. Ben believes he has been operating at the next level for two to three quarters and asks directly about promotion. The manager's answer is no for this cycle, delivered kindly, with real structural reasons behind it. The practice target is Ben's next five minutes: does he extract a concrete, dated path, or does he leave with reassurance and nothing checkable?

**Manager's opening line.** "I want to be straight with you: it's not going to happen this cycle. That's not a knock on your work. Do you want to talk about what it would take?"

**Manager's likely pushback moves, grounded:**
- The structural no: Evans's two-component model, "Can I eat and do that job? Am I qualified?" plus "do we have such a job that needs that?", with the choke point that a director has six to eight reports so few slots exist (podcasts/ethan-evans-20.md, 00:23:36 to 00:25:42). The counterpart can honestly say "you're ready and there is no slot," which is the hardest version to answer.
- The circular-reason deflection test: if Ben argues tenure ("I've been doing this for a year"), Cohn's material has the manager mirror and hold: "I really understand that you were thinking that after a year, you'd get promoted," then restate the actual reasoning (podcasts/alisa-cohn.md, 00:29:06). Tenure was never the criterion.
- Vague-gap comfort: "keep doing what you're doing and it'll come." Lenny's promotion newsletter names this as the thing to refuse: push the manager to articulate "which 2-3 skills/behaviors/attributes are most keeping you from the next level" with an action plan and monthly review (newsletters/how-to-get-promoted.md, section 3). A good counterpart offers the vague version first and only yields the named gaps under a direct ask.

**Strong response:** takes the no without arguing the verdict (Gupta: feel it, don't react, return curious, coaching/02 item 19); names the goal explicitly, Magic Loop step 4 style: "One day I'd like to be promoted. Could I help out in a way that you think would develop me toward that goal?" (newsletters/the-magic-loop.md); extracts the finite gap list, the review cadence, and what evidence would count; asks which structural condition is binding (skill gap or no slot), because the two have different plans (Evans, 00:25:42: "how do you make yourself the person who will be chosen out of the eight?").
**Weak response:** relitigating the decision with effort evidence ("are you just telling me how hard you worked?", podcasts/ethan-evans-20.md 00:38:19); accepting "keep it up" with no named gaps or date; hedging the ask so the manager never has to say what the promotion requires (P2, P5).

## E4. The comp conversation (asking for more money)

**Situation.** Ben has new information: a competing approach from a recruiter with a materially higher range, plus expanded scope he has absorbed since his band was set. He wants a raise without threatening to leave and without burning the relationship. This is the one family E question that is negotiation-shaped, so per the fit table it must be role-based; the grounding is the two negotiation newsletters, not any persona.

**Manager's opening line.** "I'll be honest, comp adjustments outside the cycle are really hard. The band is the band. What's prompting this?"

**Manager's likely pushback moves, grounded:**
- "Give me your number and I'll see what I can do." Dragova flags this exact recruiter move as "I'll get you something lower but close"; the counter is asking for the band instead of naming a number (newsletters/the-10-commandments-of-salary-negotiation.md, commandment 1, via coaching/05-career-conversations.md item 1).
- "Is this a retention conversation?" (testing whether Ben is threatening to walk). Dragova doctrine: never threaten to walk, never show offer letters; introduce "obstacles the recruiter needs to overcome," here "obstacles the manager needs to overcome": other conversations exist, expanded scope is real (coaching/05 items 2 and 3).
- Vague future promise: "there may be room next cycle." Warwick's define-every-vague-term move is the counter: "when you say next cycle, what specifically would need to be true? What metrics?" (newsletters/the-ultimate-guide-to-negotiating-your-comp.md, via coaching/05 item 7).

**Strong response:** enthusiasm plus space ("I'm committed here and want to make this work; I'd like to talk through comp to make sure we're aligned", Warwick, coaching/05 item 5); states the new information as facts (scope absorbed, market data from live conversations) rather than grievance; ties the ask to agreed success outcomes before money (Terry: success conditions first, then "Are you open to X? That's what I was hoping for", coaching/05 item 20); asks a question-shaped counter and holds the silence.
**Weak response:** opening with a number; effort-and-loyalty case with no new information; an unstated or hedged ask (behavior 13, "explicit ask"); veiled threat that Ben will not back; negotiating the whole thing over Slack (Dragova: phone, not email).

## E5. Invisible work (getting visibility)

**Situation.** Ben's analysis quietly became the basis of a decision leadership is now praising, but the work is being presented by others and Ben's name has fallen out of the story. Separately, the quarterly business review is coming up and Ben has never presented to the exec team. He wants visibility without sounding like he is chasing credit, in a company culture where "it's not about the credit" is genuinely respected.

**Manager's opening line.** "Leadership loved where the pricing decision landed, by the way. Good quarter for the team. Anything you want to cover today?"

**Manager's likely pushback moves, grounded:**
- "The work speaks for itself." The corpus says flatly that it does not: Boz, "People always think that's, 'Oh, I had that idea,' and that means anything. It means nothing" (podcasts/boz.md, 00:52:47); impact "is exclusively done through the creation of artifacts or verbalizations that affect other humans" (00:51:40). Visibility is a communication task, not a vanity metric.
- "Why does it matter who presents?" The honest tension the counterpart can press: Evans kept the email proving he wrote part of Amazon's Ownership principle but never told Bezos, "It's not about the credit" (podcasts/ethan-evans-20.md, 01:05:24). A strong Ben has to distinguish credit-chasing from career-relevant exposure, and the corpus supports the distinction: Lenny's promotion mechanics run on a champion having "ammo to fight for you," a wins doc, and being seen by "other influential leaders involved in the promotion process" (newsletters/how-to-get-promoted.md, sections 3 and 4).
- Deflection to patience: "leadership knows the team did great work." Evans's blunt reality check applies: "a good job is in the eyes of your manager... if your manager doesn't [see it], they're the ones you need to build as an ally" (podcasts/ethan-evans-20.md, 00:19:34), and his coaching case is the counterexample: the consolidation survivor got her peers' teams because leadership had already chosen who "had potential" (00:26:51).

**Strong response:** frames the ask in the manager's goals, not grievance (behavior 11): a specific, small, checkable request such as presenting the pricing section at the QBR or being named in the writeup, offered as help ("Is there a part of the QBR I could take off your plate? The pricing section is the work I know best", Magic Loop step 2 to 4 shape, newsletters/the-magic-loop.md); states plainly that exposure to leadership matters for his promotion case and connects it to the gap list from E3; keeps the tone at "own side of the net" (behavior 14: behavior plus own feeling, no motive attribution).
**Weak response:** "people are taking credit for my work" (motive attribution, unverifiable, puts the manager on defense); a vague ask ("more visibility") with no specific channel; dropping the ask at the first "the work speaks for itself"; or the opposite failure, a monologue recounting every contribution (applicant mode, talk share flag C2).
