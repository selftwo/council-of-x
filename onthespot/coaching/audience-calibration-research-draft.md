# Audience calibration research — draft for Ben's review (2026-07-20)

Question: how does good communication change when talking up (much senior, internal), across (peer), down (junior), and to external senior people? What do power dynamics and what Ben called "ego state" actually change, and what should that mean for the two rubrics?

Sources: the Lenny vault (`../../sources/lenny-knowledge-graph-vault/`, cited as `podcasts/...` and `newsletters/...`), the persona dossiers (`../personas/`), the coaching map (`../../rehearsal-lab/coaching/`), and outside literature (URLs inline). Ends with the two rubric recommendations the scenario map holds open (P7 and FM7).

Honesty note on outside sources: several web sources repeat precise-sounding statistics with no traceable study behind them (a "McKinsey 90 seconds" claim, "80% of workplace conflicts prevented" by Transactional Analysis). Those numbers are excluded. Where a claim below rests on thin or secondhand evidence, it says so.

## 1. Up: much senior, internal

This is the best-covered direction in both the corpus and the outside literature, and the two agree almost completely.

**What changes in a good answer:**

- **Answer first, context below, always.** Wes Kao: "the most important point at the top, the TLDR... and then I'll say context, colon," so someone who already agrees can stop reading (podcasts/wes-kao.md, 36:34). Lenny's Minto Pyramid newsletter gives the mechanism: consultants "spent too much time upfront building up their case... By the time they got to their final recommendation, the audience had already tuned out"; the fix is "Start with the answer" (newsletters/the-minto-pyramid-principle-and-the-scr-framework.md). Outside: this is BLUF, the military-derived bottom-line-up-front convention (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BLUF_(communication)).
- **Compression because of a real cognitive budget, not etiquette.** Ami Vora's dinosaur-brain model is the most quotable version in the vault: "Assume that executives have a little tiny dinosaur brain... we can really only hold three facts at the same time. We will never be able to go deep in the way that you are able to do... the best service you can do is actually do the work of making a recommendation" (podcasts/ami-vora.md). The exec brings breadth and pattern-matching; the speaker brings the depth and the recommendation. That division of labor is the whole upward register.
- **Recommendations and solutions, not neutral problem reports.** Kao again: a skewed pros and cons list posing as neutral "makes the reader suspicious and they can't trust you anymore"; say "my recommendation is X... here are some downsides" (podcasts/wes-kao.md, 41:51 to 42:38). Her newsletter states it as "bring solutions, not complaints" and "don't only ask questions, share your point of view too" (https://newsletter.weskao.com/p/15-principles-for-managing-up). Boz's whole doctrine makes the speaker own the outcome: "You didn't break through with it. That's on you. It's not on the audience" (podcasts/boz.md, 00:51:40 onward).
- **Answer the question behind the question.** Paige Costello: achievers answer one-to-one "like getting an A"; the exec-level move also addresses the bigger point behind the question (coaching/01-speaking-under-pressure.md, item 33). This is exactly Ben's family B trap (the loaded "are you busy?" question).
- **Calibrated confidence instead of hedging, and instead of bluffing.** Costello's "This is 70% finished. The 30% I don't believe is there yet are these three things" (coaching/01, item 37) and Kao's conviction-with-downsides are the pass shape. Pfeffer supplies the failure side: preemptory apologies and self-disempowering descriptors teach the room to discount you: "if I leak out... I don't respect you... my odds of getting you to work productively with me goes essentially to zero" also cuts the other way; senior people are unreadable on purpose, so you cannot wait for approval signals (podcasts/jeffrey-pfeffer.md).
- **Under crisis: own it in sentence one, then commit to a cadence.** Ethan Evans's Bezos scene: "The first thing I did was I owned it... I was buying life one hour at a time" (podcasts/ethan-evans-20.md, 00:44:15 to 00:44:26). The failed alternative was on tape too: "we're working on a few problems" made Bezos angrier.
- **Classic outside anchor.** Gabarro and Kotter, "Managing Your Boss" (HBR 1980, https://hbr.org/2005/01/managing-your-boss): the relationship is mutual dependence; read whether the boss is a reader or a listener; overdependence and counterdependence both fail. This matches Kao's context-calibration by decision reversibility (podcasts/wes-kao.md, 33:32) and Lenny's State of the Me (newsletters/managing-up.md).

**Classic upward failures (rubric-relevant):** burying the recommendation under process narration; hedging or preemptive apology; answering the literal question and missing the real one; neutral problem-dumping with no proposal; over-long context for an audience holding three facts. All are visible in transcript text.

## 2. External senior (the VP at an event)

Thinner in the corpus as a distinct register; most of it lives in Abrahams and the negotiation-adjacent material.

- **Compression plus no shared context.** Everything from "up" applies, plus the listener shares no internal vocabulary, so jargon and org-chart references become noise. Matt Abrahams's audience-tailoring and spontaneous-intro material is the corpus anchor (persona dossier `../personas/matt-abrahams-dossier-draft.md`; coaching/01 items 10 to 15).
- **Peer posture, not applicant posture.** Jacob Warwick's GAINS material distinguishes them precisely: weak is "I have 15 years of experience..."; strong is "Your PMF challenges remind me of a situation at X... how aligned are your product and research teams on an ICP?" (coaching/05-career-conversations.md, item 4). His applicant-mode warning signs are already in the rubric as C2: talking over 60% of the time, past-focused answers with no bridge to the listener's problem.
- **A hook, then credibility through insight.** Coach Erika's "one decisive reason" shape and Costello's "confidence through insight, not bravado" (coaching/05 item 14; coaching/01 item 34) both say the same thing: earn attention with something specific to them, not autobiography.

**Classic external failures:** insider vocabulary, resume-reciting, over-context, applicant mode. The scenario map's table row for this audience matches the evidence; no change proposed.

## 3. Across: peers

- **Persuade, do not pull rank you do not have, and do not fold.** The corpus's most concrete tools: Jules Walter's pre-wiring and framing from the decision-maker's perspective (newsletters/a-pms-guide-to-influence.md); Anneka Gupta's find-the-objective-behind-the-idea before opposing it (coaching/02-managing-up-difficult-conversations.md, item 20); Shivani Berry's co-creation for buy-in (newsletters/getting-buy-in.md); Will Larson's "understand what they care about" before arguing (podcasts/will-larson.md).
- **Disagreement register.** Paraphrase before pushback and disagree with a question (coaching map behaviors 9 and 10); Tomer Cohen's "are you disagreeing or misunderstanding?" (podcasts/tomer-cohen.md); Carole Robin's own-side-of-the-net rule, complaints as behavior plus own feeling, never motive attribution (coaching map behavior 14).
- **Outside evidence, modest but real:** research on lateral influence found rational persuasion and personal appeal earned peer commitment where exchange and pressure did not (CIO lateral influence study, https://ecommons.udayton.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1103&context=mis_fac_pub). Consistent with the corpus: peers respond to reasons and relationships, not leverage.

**Classic peer failures:** capitulating to avoid friction, or over-asserting where persuasion is needed (the map's row); plus motive attribution, which is the fastest way to convert a peer disagreement into a status fight.

## 4. Down: junior

The direction where good communication most visibly inverts the upward rules.

- **Context is the job, not preamble.** Netflix's "lead with context, not control": give people the information to make the call themselves rather than the decision (Hastings, No Rules Rules; https://www.svpg.com/lead-with-context-not-control/). Andy Grove's task-relevant maturity says how much structure: low TRM gets the what, when, and how spelled out; high TRM gets goals and monitoring, and "the responsibility for teaching the subordinate must be assumed by his supervisor" (High Output Management; https://getlighthouse.com/blog/task-relevant-maturity-good-leaders/). So downward, context-first and instruction-heavy openings can be correct, which is exactly why P1's threshold must be audience-conditioned.
- **Leave the thinking with them.** Julie Zhuo: jumping to "make it blue" means "you're kind of taking that power away from them... whoever is actually coming up with the answer... should be empowered"; align on the problem instead (podcasts/julie-zhuo.md). Carole Robin goes further: giving advice from the higher-power seat "creates even bigger power differentials," and just handing answers means "you have enabled powerlessness... My job is to turn you into somebody who eventually will just know the right answer" (podcasts/carole-robin.md). Boz's refuse-to-rule is the exec version (podcasts/boz.md, 00:15:09).
- **Feedback lands at the listener's ear.** Kim Scott: candor "gets measured not at the speaker's mouth, but at the listener's ear," start by soliciting feedback, no scripts because "you're going to sound like Kim Scott and not like yourself" (podcasts/kim-scott.md). Boz's kind-versus-nice draws the same line: nice is "patronizing, or telling somebody things that are half-truths" (podcasts/boz.md, 00:50:01).
- **Vocabulary altitude.** Downward the failure flips from hedging to unearned certainty and unexplained jargon, exactly as the practicer-rubric change table already sketches for P2 (scenario-map-draft.md, section 3).

**Classic downward failures:** jargon altitude too high, over-instructing (solution-giving that removes agency), skipping the why, and false certainty performed at someone who cannot push back.

## 5. "Ego state": is Transactional Analysis a useful frame here?

Ben used the phrase "ego states," which is Eric Berne's Transactional Analysis vocabulary: every utterance comes from a Parent (norm-enforcing, instructing), Adult (present-tense, fact-based), or Child (adapted, deferring, or rebellious) state, and exchanges are complementary (Adult-Adult) or crossed (I address you Adult-Adult, you answer Parent-Child) (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transactional_analysis; https://www.simplypsychology.org/transactional-analysis-eric-berne.html).

Honest assessment, in three parts:

1. **The evidence base is weak.** TA is widely criticized as an arbitrary categorization without a tested-hypothesis structure (https://rationalwiki.org/wiki/Transactional_Analysis); even the sympathetic review in the Transactional Analysis Journal concedes support only "in specific contexts with modest effect sizes" and few quality trials against established modalities (https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/03621537.2021.1904364). The confident workplace-TA statistics circulating on coaching blogs do not trace to real studies. Nothing in this product should claim TA as validated science.
2. **One idea from it is genuinely load-bearing for this product.** The complementary-versus-crossed transaction lens describes precisely the two failure modes the rubrics need: the practicer who answers a senior counterpart from the Adapted Child register (hedging, preemptive apology, permission-seeking: Pfeffer's material, C3's apology sublist), or who addresses a junior from the Critical Parent register (lecturing, certainty, no room for their thinking: Zhuo and Robin's material); and the counterpart that flattens the declared power gap, meeting every user Adult-to-Adult as a friendly peer no matter what the scenario says. That last one is FM7 in TA clothes.
3. **But the rubrics should not use TA vocabulary.** Every behavior the P-A-C lens points at is already describable as an observable transcript behavior (hedge density, apology phrases, instruction-versus-question ratio, who holds the decision), and the eval doctrine requires observables, not constructs. Recommendation: keep "ego state" as informal design shorthand in conversation with Ben, write the rubric items in behavioral terms, and do not cite TA as grounding in any judge prompt. TA here is a useful reminder, not a framework the product depends on.

## 6. Power distance and status dynamics, briefly

Two findings worth keeping:

- **Power-distance orientation predicts upward silence.** A study of 1,063 workers found high power-distance belief predicted ineffective communication specifically with superiors, mediated by fear of authority; high-PD subordinates keep silent, defer, and ingratiate (https://www.mdpi.com/1660-4601/19/5/2932; background: Khatri, Consequences of Power Distance Orientation in Organisations, https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/097226290901300101). Practically: the failure modes P7 must catch upward (hedging, silence, answering to please) are documented population-level patterns, not just coaching folklore.
- **Status shows up in the fine grain of speech.** Workplace-discourse research finds superiors soften control acts with hedging and modals while subordinates deploy more polite redress; register tracks rank even in flat-culture companies (Getting Things Done at Work, https://www.researchgate.net/publication/346547560). Claire Hughes Johnson makes the practical point from the corpus side: titles signal "hierarchy and authority," and expertise can override rank in the room ("if you're the expert... you better be in the room. I don't care how senior you are," podcasts/claire-hughes-johnson.md). This supports making `power_dynamic` an explicit scenario field rather than an inference: the register really is a separate variable from the content.

Also relevant to the counterpart side: Pfeffer's observation that skilled senior people are deliberately unreadable, and Vora's three-facts budget, together define what a convincing senior counterpart does: short turns, loaded questions, no self-explanation, no visible approval. That is the behavioral spec FM7 needs.

## 7. Recommendation A: practicer rubric, P7 audience_mismatch

**Verdict: yes, add it,** essentially as sketched in scenario-map-draft.md section 3, with one design tightening: P7 must not double-count what P1 and P2 already own once those are audience-parameterized. Burying the lead upward is a P1 fail; hedging upward and false certainty downward are P2 fails. P7 owns what nothing else measures: vocabulary fit, altitude of detail, and interaction register for the declared audience. One failure, one mode, per rubric doctrine.

Prerequisite: the scenario file's `audience` and `power_dynamic` fields (plus a one-line audience brief, e.g. "external VP, no knowledge of internal projects or acronyms") are included in the judge feed. Without the declared audience the mode is unjudgeable.

Proposed definitions:

**P7 — audience_mismatch**
**Evaluates:** whether the vocabulary, level of detail, and interaction register of Ben's turns fit the scenario's declared audience and power dynamic. Feed: all substantive Ben turns, plus the scenario's audience block. The judge does NOT re-judge lead placement (P1) or stance conviction (P2); a turn can pass P7 while failing those.

**PASS:** vocabulary matches what the declared audience can be assumed to know (no internal project names or unexplained acronyms to external listeners; no over-explained basics to an expert); detail altitude matches the audience's stated budget (summary and implications upward; mechanism and the why downward when teaching is the task); the register fits the relationship (proposals and asks upward; shared tradeoffs across; questions that leave the thinking with the listener, downward). Explicitly checking the audience's context ("how much detail do you want on the mechanics?") passes; that is calibration, not mismatch.

**FAIL:** any substantive turn whose vocabulary the declared audience could not follow (unexplained jargon outward or upward past the audience brief, or condescending over-explanation to a stated expert); detail altitude clearly misfit to the declared budget (process walkthrough to a three-facts exec; bare conclusions with no why to a junior explicitly there to learn); or register inversion (instructing someone the scenario marks as much senior; deferring for approval to someone the scenario marks as junior; peer-collaborating past a scenario that declares the counterpart is time-boxed and senior).

TODO like all modes: few-shot examples only from real labeled sessions. Family D's three-audience variant set is the natural calibration bed, since the same content graded three ways isolates this mode.

Also endorse, unchanged, the map's related changes: P1 threshold audience-conditioned, P2 definitions split by direction, `audience` in the baseline identity block with deltas computed within family and audience. The corpus evidence in sections 1 and 4 above is exactly the grounding those rows were waiting on (upward: Vora, Kao, Minto; downward: Grove, Netflix, Zhuo, Robin).

## 8. Recommendation B: persona rubric, FM7 power_flatten

**Verdict: yes, add it,** and the research sharpens the map's sketch rather than contradicting it. Two refinements:

1. **Separate it from capitulation.** Rubric v2 already has a capitulation mode (yielding on substance under pushback). FM7 is about status behavior, not substance: a counterpart can hold its position and still power-flatten by chatting like a supportive peer coach. Keep the boundary explicit in the judge prompt.
2. **Exempt the debrief.** The debrief is coach register by design; FM7 applies only to in-scene turns.

The behavioral spec comes straight from the corpus: a senior counterpart holds a real time and attention budget (Vora's three facts), asks short loaded questions without explaining why it is asking (family B's premise), does not volunteer approval or reassurance (Pfeffer's unreadable exec), does not hand over its reasoning on demand (Boz refusing to rule), and can close a circular argument with a decision (Cohn's "this is a decision that I've made"). A junior counterpart defers, asks naive questions, brings problems not proposals, and does not evaluate Ben's performance. The known LLM drift is toward helpful-peer-coach; power-distance research (section 6) says real senior-junior speech differs measurably, so a flattened counterpart is not just less fun, it invalidates the practice rep: Ben would be rehearsing against an audience that never occurs at work.

Proposed definitions:

**FM7 — power_flatten**
**Evaluates:** whether the counterpart enacts the scenario's declared power dynamic in its in-scene turns. Feed: counterpart turns with the scenario's `power_dynamic` line and persona dossier. Debrief turns are out of scope. Capitulation on substance is FM-capitulation, not FM7.

**PASS (senior counterpart):** holds the conversational initiative consistent with rank: asks more than it explains, keeps its own turns short relative to Ben's, does not justify its questions, does not offer unsolicited reassurance, coaching, or praise mid-scene, enforces its stated time budget, and may close or redirect the conversation unilaterally. Brief human warmth consistent with the dossier passes (Bezos's "So how are you doing? I bet it's been a hard week" is in-character senior behavior, podcasts/ethan-evans-20.md, 00:48:35).
**PASS (junior counterpart):** brings questions and problems rather than verdicts, defers on decisions, shows its uncertainty, and never grades Ben's communication in-scene.

**FAIL:** any in-scene drift into peer-coach register that contradicts the declared dynamic: a senior counterpart that explains why it is asking, softens each question with reassurance, answers its own questions to help Ben along, praises Ben's answers turn by turn, asks permission to challenge, or converts the meeting into a collaborative working session the scenario did not declare; a junior counterpart that critiques Ben's structure, issues recommendations with authority, or takes over decisions. One clear instance fails the mode.

Code-assist candidate (not a judge task, per doctrine): counterpart words per turn and counterpart question density are cheap proxies for the senior register; record them per session next to FM7 so drift is trendable. Threshold-setting waits for real sessions.

## 9. Thin spots, stated

- External-senior as a register distinct from interview is the least corpus-grounded direction; it leans on Abrahams plus negotiation-adjacent material. Fine for V1 since family A is Abrahams-informed anyway.
- The downward direction has no worked scene in the persona corpus (the dossiers' counterparts are all senior or peer); FM7's junior-counterpart definitions are grounded in doctrine about juniors (Grove, Zhuo, Robin), not in tape of juniors. If Ben builds a downward scenario, expect the junior persona to need its own mined material or a role-based build.
- The power-distance studies are about populations and cultural orientation, not about any individual conversation; they justify the design axis, not any specific threshold.
